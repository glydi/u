    Stochastic processes

    A random variable is one roll of the dice; a stochastic process is dice rolled through time — a whole random trajectory. Stock prices, queue lengths, token sequences, the noising of an image in a diffusion model: each is a family of random variables indexed by time, and the interesting questions are about the paths, not single values.

    Definition A stochastic process is a collection $\{X_t\}_{t\in T}$ of random variables on one probability space, indexed by time $T$ (discrete: $T=\{0,1,2,\dots\}$; continuous: $T=[0,\infty)$). A sample path is the function $t\mapsto X_t(\omega)$ for one outcome $\omega$. The process is stationary if all joint distributions are invariant under time shifts.
    Worked example Let $X_t=A\cos(t+\Theta)$ where $A,\Theta$ are random ($\Theta$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$, independent of $A$ with $\mathbb{E}[A^2]<\infty$). Each path is a smooth cosine; the randomness lies in amplitude and phase. Mean: $\mathbb{E}[X_t]=\mathbb{E}[A]\,\mathbb{E}[\cos(t+\Theta)]=0$ for all $t$. Autocovariance: $\mathbb{E}[X_tX_s]=\mathbb{E}[A^2]\,\mathbb{E}[\cos(t+\Theta)\cos(s+\Theta)]=\tfrac12\mathbb{E}[A^2]\cos(t-s)$ — depends only on the lag: (wide-sense) stationary.

    1. EASY Classify each by index and state space: (a) daily closing price; (b) number of requests by time $t$; (c) temperature as a function of continuous time.
      Solution
      (a) discrete time, continuous state; (b) continuous time, discrete state (a counting process); (c) continuous time, continuous state.
    2. MED $X_t=Z$ for all $t$ (one Gaussian frozen in time) vs $X_t$ i.i.d. Gaussians. Both are stationary — contrast their sample paths and autocovariances.
      Solution
      Frozen: flat constant paths, $\operatorname{Cov}(X_t,X_s)=\sigma^2$ for all lags (perfect memory). i.i.d.: wildly jagged paths, $\operatorname{Cov}=0$ for $t\neq s$ (no memory). Stationarity constrains distributions, not smoothness.
    3. HARD Is the random walk $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i$ ($\xi_i$ i.i.d., mean 0, variance 1) stationary? Are its increments stationary?
      Solution
      Not stationary: $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n$ grows. But increments $S_{n+k}-S_n$ depend only on $k$ in distribution (sum of $k$ i.i.d. terms): stationary increments, and independent ones too — the defining pattern of walks and Brownian motion.

    Random walks

    Flip a coin, step left or right, repeat: the random walk is the fruit fly of stochastic processes. It spreads like $\sqrt n$, revisits the origin in 1 and 2 dimensions but escapes forever in 3, and rescaled properly it becomes Brownian motion.

    Definition Simple random walk: $S_0=0$, $S_n=\sum_{i=1}^n\xi_i$ with i.i.d. steps $\xi_i=\pm1$, $P(\xi=+1)=p$. Symmetric case $p=\tfrac12$: $\mathbb{E}[S_n]=0$, $\operatorname{Var}(S_n)=n$, and by the CLT $S_n/\sqrt n\xrightarrow{d}N(0,1)$. Pólya: recurrent in dimensions 1 and 2, transient in $\ge3$.
    Worked example Symmetric walk, 100 steps. Typical distance from start: $\sqrt{100}=10$, not 100. $P(S_{100}=0)=\binom{100}{50}2^{-100}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{50\pi}}\approx0.0796$ (Stirling): even after 100 fair steps, returning exactly to zero happens only ~8% of the time — yet return eventually is certain in 1-D.
    Explore

    Sample fresh 1-D walks and watch the $\pm\sqrt n$ envelope contain the typical spread. Nudge the bias $p$ and see drift take over.

    Where this goes Rescaled walks become Brownian motion (below); walks on graphs power PageRank and MCMC (Ch 13); gambler's-ruin logic reappears in bandits (Ch 24).

    Markov chains

    A process with amnesia: the future depends on the present state only, not on how you got there. That single assumption — the Markov property — makes everything computable with matrices, and it is the modeling backbone of queues, PageRank, MCMC, RL environments and (approximately) language.

    Definition A (discrete-time, finite-state) Markov chain has $$P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i,X_{n-1},\dots,X_0)=P(X_{n+1}=j\mid X_n=i)=P_{ij},$$ time-homogeneous transition probabilities collected in the matrix $P$ (rows sum to 1). Multi-step: $P(X_{n+m}=j\mid X_n=i)=(P^m)_{ij}$ (Chapman–Kolmogorov).
    Worked example Weather: Sunny→Sunny 0.8, Sunny→Rainy 0.2; Rainy→Sunny 0.4, Rainy→Rainy 0.6. $P=\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.4&0.6\end{pmatrix}$. Two-day forecast from Sunny: $P^2=\begin{pmatrix}0.72&0.28\\0.56&0.44\end{pmatrix}$ — $P(\text{rain in 2 days}\mid\text{sunny now})=0.28$. Note the rows already drifting toward each other: memory of today fading.
    Pitfall "Markov" is relative to the chosen state. A process that looks non-Markov (yesterday matters too) becomes Markov if you enlarge the state to (today, yesterday). Modeling skill is choosing a state that carries just enough memory — the same idea as the state in RL (Ch 25).

    2. MED From Sunny, what is the probability of the exact sequence Sunny, Sunny, Rainy, Rainy over the next three days?
      Solution

    3. HARD A mouse moves between rooms $\{1,2,3\}$ arranged in a line (from 1 or 3 it must move to 2; from 2 it moves to 1 or 3 equally). Write $P$, find $P^2$, and explain the period-2 behavior.
      Solution
      $P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\ \tfrac12&0&\tfrac12\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $P^2=\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac12&0&\tfrac12\\0&1&0\\ \tfrac12&0&\tfrac12\end{pmatrix}$. The chain alternates between $\{2\}$ and $\{1,3\}$: period 2, so $P^n$ never converges (it oscillates), although a stationary distribution $(\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac14)$ exists. Periodicity is one of the two obstructions to convergence (the other: disconnected states).

    Transition matrices

    The chain's entire law in one matrix: row $i$ is the next-state distribution from state $i$. Evolution of beliefs is a vector–matrix product, $n$-step behavior is a matrix power, and the long-run fate of the chain is an eigenvalue question (Ch 8, meet Ch 11).

    Definition $P\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times S}$ is row-stochastic: $P_{ij}\ge0$, $\sum_jP_{ij}=1$. If the row vector $\boldsymbol\mu_n$ is the distribution at time $n$, then $\boldsymbol\mu_{n+1}=\boldsymbol\mu_nP$ and $\boldsymbol\mu_n=\boldsymbol\mu_0P^n$. Eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda|\le1$, and $\lambda=1$ always (eigenvector $\mathbf 1$ on the right).
    Worked example Weather chain, start 50/50: $\boldsymbol\mu_1=(0.5,0.5)P=(0.6,0.4)$; $\boldsymbol\mu_2=(0.6,0.4)P=(0.64,0.36)$; $\boldsymbol\mu_3=(0.656,0.344)$ — marching toward $(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)$. Eigenvalues of $P$: $1$ and $0.8+0.6-1=0.4$; the gap $1-0.4$ sets the convergence rate: error shrinks by 0.4× per step.
    Drill
    1. EASY Which are valid transition matrices? $A=\begin{pmatrix}0.5&0.5\\0.3&0.6\end{pmatrix}$, $B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
      Solution
      $A$: no (second row sums to 0.9). $B$: yes (a deterministic swap).
    2. MED Show that the product of two row-stochastic matrices is row-stochastic.
      Solution
      Nonnegativity is clear. Rows: $\sum_j(PQ)_{ij}=\sum_j\sum_kP_{ik}Q_{kj}=\sum_kP_{ik}\sum_jQ_{kj}=\sum_kP_{ik}=1$. Hence $P^n$ is a legitimate $n$-step transition matrix.
    3. HARD Prove every eigenvalue of a row-stochastic $P$ satisfies $|\lambda|\le1$.
      Solution
      Let $P\mathbf v=\lambda\mathbf v$, and pick $i$ with $|v_i|$ maximal. Then $|\lambda||v_i|=|\sum_jP_{ij}v_j|\le\sum_jP_{ij}|v_j|\le|v_i|\sum_jP_{ij}=|v_i|$, so $|\lambda|\le1$. (Gershgorin's take: all rows have center $P_{ii}$ and radius $1-P_{ii}$.)

    Stationary distributions

    Some belief vectors don't move: run the chain one step and the distribution comes back unchanged. That fixed point $\boldsymbol\pi=\boldsymbol\pi P$ is where the chain spends its long-run time — the resting profile of a queue, the PageRank of the web, the target density of an MCMC sampler.

    Definition $\boldsymbol\pi$ is stationary if $\boldsymbol\pi P=\boldsymbol\pi$, $\pi_i\ge0$, $\sum_i\pi_i=1$ — a left eigenvector for $\lambda=1$. For finite, irreducible chains it exists and is unique, and $\pi_i=1/\mathbb{E}_i[\text{return time to }i]$. Detailed balance ($\pi_iP_{ij}=\pi_jP_{ji}$ for all $i,j$) implies stationarity and is the design principle of MCMC.
    Worked example Weather chain: solve $(\pi_S,\pi_R)\begin{pmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.4&0.6\end{pmatrix}=(\pi_S,\pi_R)$: $0.8\pi_S+0.4\pi_R=\pi_S\Rightarrow0.4\pi_R=0.2\pi_S\Rightarrow\pi_S=2\pi_R$. Normalize: $\boldsymbol\pi=(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)$: two sunny days for every rainy one, whatever today looks like. Check detailed balance: $\tfrac23\cdot0.2=\tfrac{2}{15}=\tfrac13\cdot0.4$ ✓ (two-state chains always satisfy it).

    A stationary distribution $\boldsymbol\pi$ of a chain $P$ is…
    It is a distribution fixed by the dynamics (left eigenvector for $\lambda=1$, normalized, nonnegative). The chain keeps moving between states; it's the probability profile that stands still.

    Ergodicity

    When is "time average = space average"? An ergodic chain forgets its start and visits states in proportion to $\boldsymbol\pi$, so one long trajectory reveals the whole stationary law. This is precisely the property MCMC banks on: simulate long enough and sample averages converge to expectations under the target.

    Definition A finite chain is irreducible (every state reachable from every other) and aperiodic (return times to some state have gcd 1) ⟹ ergodic: $P(X_n=j\mid X_0=i)\to\pi_j$ for all $i,j$, and for any function $f$, $$\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^Nf(X_n)\ \longrightarrow\ \sum_i\pi_if(i)\quad\text{almost surely (ergodic theorem).}$$
    Worked example The weather chain is irreducible (both states reachable) and aperiodic ($P_{SS}=0.8>0$ gives period 1): ergodic. Simulating $10^5$ days from any start, the fraction of sunny days converges to $\tfrac23$ — one run suffices, no ensemble needed. Counterexamples: the mouse chain (periodic — averages converge but $P^n$ oscillates), and a chain with two disconnected islands (time average depends on where you start).
    Drill
    1. EASY Is the deterministic swap $B=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ ergodic?
      Solution
      Irreducible yes, but period 2: not ergodic; $P^n$ alternates and never converges (time averages still hit $(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$).
    2. MED Give the standard one-line fix that makes any irreducible chain aperiodic, and why it preserves $\boldsymbol\pi$... does it?
      Solution
      Lazy chain: $P'=\tfrac12(I+P)$ — flip a coin, stay or move. Self-loops kill periodicity; and $\boldsymbol\pi P'=\tfrac12\boldsymbol\pi+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\pi P=\boldsymbol\pi$: same stationary distribution, at half the speed.
    3. HARD An MCMC practitioner runs one chain for $10^6$ steps but the target has two modes separated by a region the proposal essentially never crosses. Relate to ergodicity and explain the failure mode.
      Solution
      Formally the chain is irreducible (crossing has tiny positive probability), so the ergodic theorem applies — but its convergence time exceeds any feasible run: practical non-ergodicity. The trajectory explores one mode; time averages estimate the wrong (conditional) distribution with deceptively small error bars. Remedies: tempering, better proposals, multiple chains with dispersed starts and $\hat R$ diagnostics (Ch 13); the quantitative version is mixing time (next).

    Mixing time

    Ergodicity says the chain converges; mixing time asks how fast — how many steps until the distribution is within ε of stationary, from the worst start. The answer is governed by the spectral gap: the distance from the second eigenvalue to 1. Shuffling cards, MCMC burn-in, and diffusion model noising schedules are all mixing-time questions.

    Definition Total variation distance: $\|\mu-\nu\|_{TV}=\tfrac12\sum_i|\mu_i-\nu_i|$. Mixing time: $$t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)=\min\{n:\max_{i}\|P^n(i,\cdot)-\boldsymbol\pi\|_{TV}\le\epsilon\}.$$ For reversible chains with eigenvalues $1=\lambda_1>\lambda_2\ge\cdots$: convergence is geometric with rate $\lambda_\star=\max_{k\ge2}|\lambda_k|$, and $t_{\mathrm{mix}}(\epsilon)\approx\frac{\ln(1/\epsilon)+\ln(1/\pi_{\min})/2}{1-\lambda_\star}$ up to constants; $1-\lambda_\star$ is the spectral gap.
    Worked example Weather chain: $\lambda_2=0.4$, so distance to stationarity shrinks by $0.4$ per step. From Sunny: initial TV distance $\|(1,0)-(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)\|_{TV}=\tfrac13$. After $n$ steps: $\tfrac13(0.4)^n$. For $\epsilon=0.01$: $n\ge\frac{\ln(100/3)}{\ln(1/0.4)}\approx\frac{3.51}{0.916}\approx3.8$: 4 steps. A sticky chain with $\lambda_2=0.99$ would need ~350 steps for the same accuracy — gap 60× smaller, mixing ~90× slower.
    Drill
    1. EASY Two chains have spectral gaps 0.5 and 0.01. Roughly how do their mixing times compare?
      Solution
      Mixing time scales like $1/\text{gap}$: the second is ~50× slower.
    2. MED Compute $\|\mu-\nu\|_{TV}$ for $\mu=(0.7,0.2,0.1)$, $\nu=(0.3,0.3,0.4)$, and state its meaning.
      Solution
      $\tfrac12(0.4+0.1+0.3)=0.4$ — the maximum disagreement over any event: some event's probability differs by 0.4 between the two laws.
    3. HARD For the two-state chain $P=\begin{pmatrix}1-a&a\\b&1-b\end{pmatrix}$, show $\lambda_2=1-a-b$ and find the fastest-mixing choice.
      Solution
      Trace $=2-a-b=\lambda_1+\lambda_2=1+\lambda_2$, so $\lambda_2=1-a-b$. Fastest mixing: $\lambda_2=0$ at $a+b=1$ — e.g. $a=b=\tfrac12$, which reaches stationarity in one step. Overly sticky ($a+b$ small) mixes slowly; overly antsy ($a+b\to2$) oscillates with $\lambda_2\to-1$ — also slow. Best is in between: a preview of step-size tuning everywhere.

    Hidden Markov models

    Often the Markov chain is backstage: you see noisy emissions, not the state itself. Speech (phonemes → audio), genomics (regions → bases), user intent (goal → clicks). The HMM's magic is that posterior beliefs over the hidden state can be updated exactly with dynamic programming — the forward algorithm, ancestor of every filtering method.

    Definition An HMM has hidden chain $Z_1\to Z_2\to\cdots$ with transitions $A_{ij}$, and observations $X_t$ emitted with $P(X_t=x\mid Z_t=i)=B_i(x)$, conditionally independent given the states. Forward algorithm: $\alpha_t(i)=P(x_{1:t},Z_t=i)$ satisfies $$\alpha_1(i)=\pi_iB_i(x_1),\qquad \alpha_{t+1}(j)=B_j(x_{t+1})\sum_i\alpha_t(i)A_{ij},$$ giving the likelihood $P(x_{1:T})=\sum_i\alpha_T(i)$ in $O(TS^2)$. (Viterbi: same recursion with max — the most likely state path.)
    Worked example Hidden weather (Sunny/Rainy, the usual $A$, start $(\tfrac23,\tfrac13)$); you only see your friend carry an umbrella: $P(\text{umb}\mid R)=0.9$, $P(\text{umb}\mid S)=0.2$. Observe umbrella on day 1: $\alpha_1(S)=\tfrac23\cdot0.2=0.1\overline{3}$, $\alpha_1(R)=\tfrac13\cdot0.9=0.3$. Posterior: $P(R\mid\text{umb})=\frac{0.3}{0.4\overline{3}}\approx0.692$. Umbrella again on day 2: $\alpha_2(S)=0.2(0.1333\cdot0.8+0.3\cdot0.4)=0.0453$; $\alpha_2(R)=0.9(0.1333\cdot0.2+0.3\cdot0.6)=0.186$. $P(R_2\mid\text{umb,umb})=\frac{0.186}{0.231}\approx0.804$ — evidence accumulating through time, exactly.
    Where this goes The same message-passing becomes the Kalman filter for linear-Gaussian states (Ch 28) and the particle filter beyond; POMDPs (Ch 26) bolt decisions onto the belief state.
    Drill
    1. EASY In an HMM, are the observations $X_1,X_2,\dots$ themselves a Markov chain?
      Solution
      No, in general. Given $X_t$, earlier observations still carry information about $Z_t$ (hence about $X_{t+1}$): the observed process has long-range dependence — one reason HMMs are more expressive than plain chains.
    2. MED Why does naive enumeration of $P(x_{1:T})$ cost $O(S^T)$, and how does the forward algorithm cut it to $O(TS^2)$?
      Solution
      Enumeration sums over all $S^T$ hidden paths. The forward recursion pushes the sum inside: at each step only the current state matters (Markov property), so we carry $S$ numbers $\alpha_t(\cdot)$ and update them with an $S\times S$ matrix — dynamic programming exploiting conditional independence.
    3. HARD Continue the worked example: day 3, NO umbrella. Compute the filtered posterior $P(R_3\mid\text{umb,umb,no})$.
      Solution
      Emissions: $P(\text{no}\mid S)=0.8$, $P(\text{no}\mid R)=0.1$. $\alpha_3(S)=0.8(\alpha_2(S)0.8+\alpha_2(R)0.4)=0.8(0.0453\cdot0.8+0.186\cdot0.4)=0.8(0.03624+0.0744)=0.0885$. $\alpha_3(R)=0.1(0.0453\cdot0.2+0.186\cdot0.6)=0.1(0.00906+0.1116)=0.01207$. Posterior: $\frac{0.01207}{0.1006}\approx0.12$ — one sunny observation sharply flips the belief back.

    Poisson process

    Pure randomness in continuous time: events arriving one by one, at constant average rate, with no memory and no coordination. Counts in any window are Poisson; gaps between events are exponential; disjoint windows are independent. It is the null model of arrivals against which all clustering and burstiness are measured.

    Definition $\{N(t)\}$ is a Poisson process with rate $\lambda$ if $N(0)=0$, increments over disjoint intervals are independent, and $N(t+s)-N(s)\sim\mathrm{Poi}(\lambda t)$. Equivalent construction: inter-arrival times i.i.d. Exp($\lambda$). Superposition: independent processes with rates $\lambda_1,\lambda_2$ merge into one with $\lambda_1+\lambda_2$; thinning: keeping each event independently with prob $p$ gives rate $p\lambda$.
    Worked example Support tickets arrive as a Poisson process, $\lambda=4$/hour. (a) $P(\text{exactly 2 in 30 min})$: $N\sim$Poi(2): $e^{-2}\frac{2^2}{2}=2e^{-2}\approx0.271$. (b) Given 6 tickets arrived in an hour, the arrival times are distributed as 6 i.i.d. uniforms on the hour, sorted — total randomness, no rush hour. (c) Wait for the next ticket at any moment: Exp(4), mean 15 min — regardless of how long it's been quiet.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\lambda=3$/min. Probability of zero events in 2 minutes?
      Solution
      $e^{-6}\approx0.0025$.
    2. MED Requests hit two servers as independent Poisson processes at 2/s and 3/s. What process do combined arrivals follow, and what fraction of events come from server 1?
      Solution
      Poisson at 5/s (superposition); each event independently comes from server 1 with probability $\frac{2}{5}$.
    3. HARD Show that for a Poisson process, given $N(T)=n$, the unordered arrival times are i.i.d. Uniform$[0,T]$.
      Solution
      For disjoint sub-intervals with lengths $t_1,\dots,t_k$ summing to $T$ and counts $n_1,\dots,n_k$ summing to $n$: $P(\text{counts}\mid N(T)=n)=\frac{\prod_ie^{-\lambda t_i}(\lambda t_i)^{n_i}/n_i!}{e^{-\lambda T}(\lambda T)^n/n!}=\binom{n}{n_1\cdots n_k}\prod_i\left(\frac{t_i}{T}\right)^{n_i}$ — the multinomial of $n$ i.i.d. uniforms. The rate cancels: conditioned on the count, a Poisson process is uniform dust.

    Martingales

    A martingale is a fair game: given everything so far, tomorrow's expected value is today's value. No strategy based on the past can generate expected profit — and that "you can't beat fairness" principle, made precise by the optional stopping theorem, yields one-line solutions to hitting problems and underlies concentration bounds like Azuma's.

    Definition $\{M_n\}$ is a martingale (w.r.t. information $\mathcal F_n$) if $\mathbb{E}|M_n|<\infty$ and $$\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n.$$ (Submartingale: $\ge$; supermartingale: $\le$.) Optional stopping: under regularity (e.g. bounded stopping time, or bounded $|M_{n}|$ up to $\tau$), $\mathbb{E}[M_\tau]=\mathbb{E}[M_0]$.
    Worked example Symmetric walk $S_n$: a martingale ($\mathbb{E}[S_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=S_n+\mathbb{E}[\xi]=S_n$). Also $M_n=S_n^2-n$: $\mathbb{E}[S_{n+1}^2\mid\mathcal F_n]=S_n^2+2S_n\mathbb{E}[\xi]+1=S_n^2+1$, so $\mathbb{E}[M_{n+1}\mid\mathcal F_n]=M_n$ ✓. Application: expected time for the walk from $k$ to hit $\{0,N\}$: optional stopping on $M_n$ gives $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau^2]-\mathbb{E}[\tau]=k^2$, and $\mathbb{E}[S_\tau^2]=N^2\cdot\frac{k}{N}=kN$ (using the ruin probability), so $\mathbb{E}[\tau]=kN-k^2=k(N-k)$. From the middle of a 100-state stretch: 2500 steps.
    Pitfall Optional stopping needs its conditions. "Double after every loss" (the martingale betting system!) targets the stopping time "first win," where $\mathbb{E}[M_\tau]\neq M_0$ because wealth is unbounded below along the way — infinite expected bankroll requirement, not free money.

    3. HARD Use De Moivre's martingale to find the ruin probability: biased walk from $k$, absorbing at 0 and $N$, $p\neq\tfrac12$.

    Brownian motion

    Shrink a random walk's steps and step-times together (steps of size $\sqrt{\Delta t}$ every $\Delta t$) and in the limit you get Brownian motion: continuous paths, independent Gaussian increments, no memory. It is the universal continuum limit of accumulated micro-randomness — pollen grains, stock noise, and the driving term of every SDE below.

    Definition Standard Brownian motion (Wiener process) $\{W_t\}_{t\ge0}$: $W_0=0$; independent increments; $W_t-W_s\sim N(0,t-s)$ for $s<t$; continuous paths. Consequences: $\mathbb{E}[W_t]=0$, $\operatorname{Var}(W_t)=t$, $\operatorname{Cov}(W_s,W_t)=\min(s,t)$; paths are nowhere differentiable; scaling: $\{cW_{t/c^2}\}$ is again Brownian.

    Ito integral

    To integrate against a path that wiggles infinitely, Riemann–Stieltjes fails. Itô's fix: evaluate the integrand at the left endpoint of each interval (no peeking at the future) and take an $L^2$ limit. The result is a martingale with a beautiful variance formula — the Itô isometry — and it is the mathematical meaning of "accumulate noise."

    Definition For adapted $H$ (using only past information) with $\mathbb{E}\int_0^TH_t^2dt<\infty$: $$\int_0^TH_t\,dW_t=\lim\sum_kH_{t_k}\,(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}).$$ Properties: $\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^TH\,dW\right]=0$; Itô isometry: $\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\int_0^TH\,dW\right)^2\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^TH_t^2\,dt\right]$; the running integral is a martingale.
    Worked example $\int_0^TW_t\,dW_t$. Guess from calculus: $\tfrac12W_T^2$. Check the mean: $\mathbb{E}[\tfrac12W_T^2]=\tfrac T2\neq0$ — but Itô integrals have mean zero! The correct value (via Itô's lemma, next) is $$\int_0^TW\,dW=\tfrac12W_T^2-\tfrac12T,$$ mean $0$ ✓. The $-\tfrac12T$ is quadratic variation's toll. Variance check by isometry: $\mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^TW_t^2dt\right]=\int_0^Tt\,dt=\tfrac{T^2}{2}$, and indeed $\operatorname{Var}(\tfrac12W_T^2-\tfrac12T)=\tfrac14\operatorname{Var}(W_T^2)=\tfrac14\cdot2T^2=\tfrac{T^2}{2}$ ✓.
    Pitfall The left-endpoint choice is not cosmetic. Evaluating at the midpoint (Stratonovich) gives a different integral ($\tfrac12W_T^2$, no correction) with different rules. Itô is the finance/filtering convention (no look-ahead, martingale property); Stratonovich obeys the ordinary chain rule and appears in physics. Know which one your SDE means.

    Ito's lemma

    The chain rule for noisy trajectories, with one crucial correction: because $(dW)^2=dt$, second derivatives contribute at first order. Itô's lemma is how you transform SDEs, derive Black–Scholes, and compute how a density's log evolves under diffusion — the identity behind score-based models.

    Theorem (Itô) If $dX_t=\mu_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t$ and $f(t,x)$ is smooth: $$df(t,X_t)=\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}+\mu_t\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}+\frac{\sigma_t^2}{2}\frac{\partial^2f}{\partial x^2}\Big)dt+\sigma_t\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,dW_t.$$ Mnemonic: Taylor-expand to second order and use $dt^2=0$, $dt\,dW=0$, $(dW)^2=dt$.
    Worked example Geometric Brownian motion: $dS=\mu S\,dt+\sigma S\,dW$. Apply Itô to $f=\ln S$: $f_x=\frac1S$, $f_{xx}=-\frac1{S^2}$: $$d\ln S=\Big(\mu S\cdot\frac1S-\frac{\sigma^2S^2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{S^2}\Big)dt+\sigma S\cdot\frac1S\,dW=\Big(\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Big)dt+\sigma\,dW.$$ So $S_t=S_0\exp\big((\mu-\tfrac{\sigma^2}{2})t+\sigma W_t\big)$: the median grows at $\mu-\frac{\sigma^2}{2}$, not $\mu$ — Jensen's volatility drag (Ch 11), now derived exactly.

    Stochastic differential equations

    An ODE with a noise term: $dX=\mu(X,t)\,dt+\sigma(X,t)\,dW$ says "drift by $\mu$, diffuse by $\sigma$." Solutions are random processes; the drift shapes where paths tend, the diffusion how much they scatter. Interest rates, molecular dynamics, SGD's noisy trajectory, and diffusion models' noising process are all SDEs.

    Definition $X_t$ solves the SDE $dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)\,dt+\sigma(X_t,t)\,dW_t$, $X_0=x_0$, if $$X_t=x_0+\int_0^t\mu(X_s,s)\,ds+\int_0^t\sigma(X_s,s)\,dW_s.$$ Existence and uniqueness hold when $\mu,\sigma$ are Lipschitz with linear growth (the stochastic Picard theorem). Euler–Maruyama scheme: $X_{n+1}=X_n+\mu(X_n,t_n)\Delta t+\sigma(X_n,t_n)\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z_n$, $Z_n\sim N(0,1)$ i.i.d.
    Worked example Simulate $dX=-X\,dt+dW$ from $X_0=2$, $\Delta t=0.5$, with noise draws $Z_1=0.3$, $Z_2=-1.1$: $X_1=2-2(0.5)+\sqrt{0.5}(0.3)=1+0.212=1.212$; $X_2=1.212-1.212(0.5)+0.707(-1.1)=0.606-0.778=-0.172$. The drift pulls toward 0, the noise keeps kicking — this is the OU process, next topic. (Note $\sqrt{\Delta t}$, not $\Delta t$, multiplies the noise: Brownian scaling.)
    Pitfall Writing the noise term as $\sigma Z\,\Delta t$ instead of $\sigma Z\sqrt{\Delta t}$ is the classic simulation bug: noise vanishes as $\Delta t\to0$ and you silently simulate the ODE.

    Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

    Brownian motion on a leash: a linear drift always pulls the state back toward a mean while noise keeps perturbing it. Unlike Brownian motion it forgets its start exponentially fast and settles into a Gaussian stationary distribution — the prototype of mean reversion, thermal equilibrium, and the noising process in variance-preserving diffusion models.

    Definition $$dX_t=\theta(m-X_t)\,dt+\sigma\,dW_t,$$ with reversion rate $\theta>0$, mean $m$, noise $\sigma$. Solution: $X_t=m+(X_0-m)e^{-\theta t}+\sigma\int_0^te^{-\theta(t-s)}dW_s$. Moments: $\mathbb{E}[X_t]=m+(X_0-m)e^{-\theta t}$; $\operatorname{Var}(X_t)=\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}(1-e^{-2\theta t})$. Stationary law: $N\!\big(m,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta}\big)$; autocorrelation decays as $e^{-\theta|\Delta t|}$.
    Worked example $\theta=2$, $m=0$, $\sigma=1$, $X_0=3$. Mean at $t=1$: $3e^{-2}\approx0.406$. Variance at $t=1$: $\frac14(1-e^{-4})\approx0.245$. So $X_1\sim N(0.406,\,0.245)$: already mostly relaxed. As $t\to\infty$: $N(0,\tfrac14)$, sd $\tfrac12$ — drift strength and noise fight to a Gaussian truce with variance $\sigma^2/2\theta$.

    Fokker-Planck equation

    Instead of tracking one noisy path, track the whole cloud: the probability density $p(x,t)$ of the SDE's state obeys a deterministic PDE. Drift advects the density, diffusion spreads it — a continuity equation (divergence theorem, Ch 9) for probability. Solve it and you know everything about the process's distribution at every time.

    Definition If $dX=\mu(x)\,dt+\sigma(x)\,dW$, the density $p(x,t)$ of $X_t$ satisfies $$\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\big[\mu(x)\,p\big]+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\big[\sigma^2(x)\,p\big].$$ Equivalently $\partial_tp=-\partial_xJ$ with probability flux $J=\mu p-\frac12\partial_x(\sigma^2p)$: total probability is conserved. Stationary density: set $J=0$; for constant $\sigma$, $p_\infty(x)\propto\exp\!\big(\frac{2}{\sigma^2}\int^x\mu(u)\,du\big)$.
    Worked example Pure diffusion ($\mu=0$, $\sigma$ const): $\partial_tp=\frac{\sigma^2}{2}\partial_x^2p$ — the heat equation (Ch 10)! From a point mass at 0, $p(x,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2t}}e^{-x^2/2\sigma^2t}$: the heat kernel = the law of Brownian motion, one identity two chapters in the making. OU check ($\mu=-\theta x$): $p_\infty\propto e^{-\theta x^2/\sigma^2}$ — the stationary Gaussian $N(0,\frac{\sigma^2}{2\theta})$ ✓.
    Where this goes For a potential $U$ with $\mu=-\nabla U$: $p_\infty\propto e^{-2U/\sigma^2}$ — the Gibbs distribution, the bridge between optimization landscapes and sampling (Langevin, next; energy-based models, Ch 22).

    Langevin dynamics

    Want samples from $p(x)\propto e^{-U(x)}$? Roll a ball downhill on $U$ while shaking it: $dX=-\nabla U\,dt+\sqrt2\,dW$. The Fokker–Planck stationary density of this SDE is exactly $p$. Discretize and you get the Langevin algorithm: gradient descent plus calibrated noise — the sampler that turns score functions into images.

    Definition Overdamped Langevin dynamics: $$dX_t=\nabla\log p(X_t)\,dt+\sqrt{2}\,dW_t$$ has stationary density $p$. Discrete (unadjusted Langevin algorithm): $$x_{k+1}=x_k+\epsilon\,\nabla\log p(x_k)+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,z_k,\quad z_k\sim N(0,I),$$ which samples approximately from $p$ for small $\epsilon$ (bias $O(\epsilon)$; adding a Metropolis accept step removes it — MALA, Ch 13). $\nabla\log p$ is the score.
    Worked example Target $N(0,1)$: $\log p=-\frac{x^2}{2}+c$, score $=-x$. Langevin step: $x_{k+1}=(1-\epsilon)x_k+\sqrt{2\epsilon}z_k$ — an AR(1) process. Its stationary variance: $v=(1-\epsilon)^2v+2\epsilon\Rightarrow v=\frac{2\epsilon}{1-(1-\epsilon)^2}=\frac{2}{2-\epsilon}\approx1+\frac{\epsilon}{2}$. At $\epsilon=0.1$: $v\approx1.053$ — close to 1, with the promised $O(\epsilon)$ bias, vanishing as $\epsilon\to0$.

    Reverse-time SDEs

    Here is the punchline the whole chapter builds to: a diffusion that gradually destroys structure can be run backwards. Anderson's theorem says the time-reversal of an SDE is another SDE whose drift needs exactly one extra ingredient — the score $\nabla\log p_t$ of the evolving density. Learn that score with a neural network and you can start from pure noise and integrate backwards into data: that is a diffusion model.

    Theorem (Anderson, 1982) If $dX_t=f(X_t,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW_t$ evolves densities $p_t$ forward for $t\in[0,T]$, then the reverse-time process (running $t$ from $T$ down to $0$) satisfies $$dX_t=\big[f(X_t,t)-g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(X_t)\big]\,dt+g(t)\,d\bar W_t,$$ with $\bar W$ a reverse-time Brownian motion. There is also a deterministic probability-flow ODE with the same marginals: $\dot x=f-\tfrac12g^2\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$.
    Worked example Forward: pure diffusion $dX=dW$ from $X_0\sim N(0,\sigma_0^2)$, so $p_t=N(0,\sigma_0^2+t)$ and $\nabla\log p_t(x)=-\frac{x}{\sigma_0^2+t}$. Reverse SDE: $dX=\frac{x}{\sigma_0^2+t}\,dt+d\bar W$ (backwards in $t$) — an anti-OU drift pushing mass back out toward the (narrower) initial law as time rewinds. Sanity: variance along the reverse flow shrinks from $\sigma_0^2+T$ back to $\sigma_0^2$, exactly undoing the forward spreading. With data instead of a Gaussian, $\nabla\log p_t$ is unknown — that is the one function the neural network must learn.
    Think first The forward process is fixed, simple, and easy to sample at any $t$ (just add Gaussian noise). Why is that a gift for training the score network?
    Reveal
    Training pairs are free: take a data point $x_0$, jump directly to $x_t=x_0+$noise (no simulation needed since Gaussian transitions are closed-form), and the network is trained so that its output matches $\nabla\log p_t(x_t\mid x_0)$, which is known analytically ($-\frac{x_t-x_0}{\sigma_t^2}$ for the VE case) — denoising score matching (Ch 22). The hard direction (generation) rides on scores learned entirely from the easy direction (noising).

(d) Autocorrelation $e^{-3\Delta t}$: time constant $\frac13$ h = 20 min — measurements 20 min apart still correlate at $e^{-1}\approx0.37$; effective independent samples come every hour or so.
    3. HARD A one-dimensional diffusion model, by hand. Data: $X_0=\pm1$ with probability ½ each. Forward noising: $X_t=X_0+\sqrt t\,Z$, $Z\sim N(0,1)$ (i.e. $dX=dW$). (a) Write $p_t(x)$. (b) Compute the score $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ and show it equals $\frac{\mathbb{E}[X_0\mid X_t=x]-x}{t}$. (c) Interpret the optimal denoiser at large vs small $t$.
      Solution
      (a) $p_t(x)=\tfrac12\varphi_t(x-1)+\tfrac12\varphi_t(x+1)$ with $\varphi_t(u)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi t}}e^{-u^2/2t}$. (b) $\partial_x\log p_t=\frac{-\frac{x-1}{t}\varphi_t(x-1)-\frac{x+1}{t}\varphi_t(x+1)}{\varphi_t(x-1)+\varphi_t(x+1)}=\frac{1}{t}\big(w_+(x)\cdot1+w_-(x)\cdot(-1)-x\big)$ where $w_\pm$ are the posterior weights of $X_0=\pm1$ given $x$; the weighted average is exactly $\mathbb{E}[X_0\mid X_t=x]=\tanh(x/t)$ here, so score $=\frac{\tanh(x/t)-x}{t}$ — score estimation ⟺ conditional-mean denoising (Tweedie's formula). (c) Large $t$: $\tanh(x/t)\approx0$ — the denoiser predicts the global mean 0; the score is $\approx-x/t$, just squashing toward the origin (coarse structure). Small $t$: $\tanh(x/t)\approx\mathrm{sign}(x)$ — the denoiser snaps to the nearest data point (fine detail). Diffusion models generate coarse-to-fine for exactly this reason.

    Task 3 — Langevin sampling of a double well Sample $p(x)\propto e^{-U(x)}$, $U=x^4/4-x^2/2$, with the unadjusted Langevin algorithm; compare the histogram to the exact (numerically normalized) density and measure the well-hopping rate.
    Reference solution
    import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(2)
U      = lambda x: x**4/4 - x**2/2
gradU  = lambda x: x**3 - x

eps, n = 0.01, 2_000_000
x, xs = 0.0, np.empty(n)
for i in range(n):
    x = x - eps*gradU(x) + np.sqrt(2*eps)*rng.standard_normal()
    xs[i] = x

# exact density on a grid
grid = np.linspace(-3, 3, 1201)
pdf = np.exp(-U(grid)); pdf /= np.trapezoid(pdf, grid)

hist, edges = np.histogram(xs[10_000:], bins=80,
                           range=(-3, 3), density=True)
centers = 0.5*(edges[1:] + edges[:-1])
err = np.abs(hist - np.interp(centers, grid, pdf)).max()
print("max density error:", err.round(3))          # small
hops = np.sum(np.sign(xs[1:]) != np.sign(xs[:-1]))
print("sign flips (well hops):", hops)
print("P(x>0):", (xs > 0).mean().round(3))          # ~0.5 by symmetry
