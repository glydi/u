    Maximum likelihood generative modeling

    A generative model is a machine that assigns probability to data and can be run backward to produce new samples. The maximum-likelihood recipe: pick a parameterized family $p_\theta$, then choose $\theta$ to make the training data as probable as possible. Every model in this chapter is a different answer to "how do we parameterize $p_\theta$ so both evaluating and sampling are tractable?"

    Definition Given data $x_1,\dots,x_n\sim p_{\text{data}}$ i.i.d., maximum likelihood solves $$\hat\theta=\arg\max_\theta\ \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p_\theta(x_i),$$ which as $n\to\infty$ minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_\theta)$ (Ch 16), since $\mathbb E[-\log p_\theta]=H(p_{\text{data}})+\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)$.
    Worked example Fit a Gaussian $p_\theta=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ to data $\{1,2,3,6\}$. MLE: $\hat\mu=\bar x=3$, $\hat\sigma^2=\frac14\sum(x_i-3)^2=\frac{4+1+0+9}{4}=3.5$. Log-likelihood at the optimum: $\sum_i\log\mathcal N(x_i;3,3.5)=-\frac42\log(2\pi\cdot3.5)-\frac{1}{2\cdot3.5}\cdot14=-2\log(21.99)-2\approx-6.182-2=-8.18$ nats. "Sampling" is then trivial: $x=3+\sqrt{3.5}\,z$, $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$.
    Pitfall KL is asymmetric. Minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)$ (what MLE does) is mass-covering: $p_\theta$ must put probability everywhere the data does, or pay infinitely — so MLE models tend to over-spread and produce blurry/implausible samples rather than miss modes. The reverse KL is mode-seeking; GANs behave more like that direction.
    Where this goes Autoregressive models, VAEs, flows and diffusion are all (exact or bounded) maximum-likelihood; Ch 23's pretraining objective is this recipe applied to text.
    Drill
    1. EASY For a Bernoulli model $p_\theta(x)=\theta^x(1-\theta)^{1-x}$ with data $(1,1,0,1)$, find the MLE.
      Solution
      $\hat\theta=$ sample mean $=3/4$. (Set derivative of $3\log\theta+\log(1-\theta)$ to zero: $3/\theta=1/(1-\theta)\Rightarrow\theta=3/4$.)
    2. MED Show that maximizing average log-likelihood is equivalent to minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)$ in the population limit.
      Solution
      $\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}[\log p_\theta(x)]=-H(p_{\text{data}})-\mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_\theta)$ by definition of KL: $\mathrm{KL}=\mathbb E[\log p_{\text{data}}-\log p_\theta]$. The entropy term is $\theta$-free, so maximizing the left side minimizes the KL.
    3. HARD Data comes from an equal mixture of $\mathcal N(-3,1)$ and $\mathcal N(3,1)$, but the model family is a single Gaussian. What does MLE converge to, and what do its samples look like?
      Solution
      MLE matches mean and variance: $\mu=0$, $\sigma^2=\mathbb E[x^2]=9+1=10$. Samples come from $\mathcal N(0,10)$: most mass lies between the true modes, where the data almost never is (density at 0 is a local minimum of the truth). Mass-covering KL forces the single Gaussian to bridge both modes — the canonical picture of why underparameterized MLE produces "blurry averages" of modes.

    Autoregressive models

    Autoregressive generative models factor the joint into a chain of next-element conditionals (Ch 21) and make each conditional a neural network's softmax. Likelihoods are exact — just multiply the conditionals — and training is stable maximum likelihood; the price is sequential sampling, one element at a time.

    Definition $$p_\theta(x)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{
    Worked example A 2-pixel binary "image" model: $p(x_1{=}1)=0.3$; $p(x_2{=}1\mid x_1{=}1)=0.9$, $p(x_2{=}1\mid x_1{=}0)=0.2$. Exact likelihood of $(1,1)$: $0.3\cdot0.9=0.27$. Full joint table: $(0,0)\!:0.7\cdot0.8=0.56$, $(0,1)\!:0.14$, $(1,0)\!:0.03$, $(1,1)\!:0.27$ — sums to 1 by construction; no normalizing constant ever needed.
    Where this goes Ch 23's LLMs are exactly this at scale; diffusion models below trade the sequential-sampling bottleneck for iterative denoising.
    Drill
    1. EASY Why does an autoregressive model need no normalizing constant?
      Solution
      Each conditional is individually normalized (softmax sums to 1), and a product of normalized conditionals is a normalized joint by the chain rule.
    2. MED A $256\times256$ RGB image has $196{,}608$ subpixels. If a PixelCNN-style model generates at 100 subpixels/second, how long does one sample take, and why can't it parallelize?
      Solution
      $196608/100\approx1966$ s $\approx33$ minutes. Each subpixel's distribution conditions on all previously generated ones, so the dependency chain is inherently sequential — the core sampling weakness that motivated flows and diffusion for images.
    3. HARD Autoregressive models over continuous values often use a mixture of discretized logistics per dimension rather than a single Gaussian. Give two reasons.
      Solution
      (1) Real conditionals are multimodal (an edge pixel is dark or bright); a unimodal Gaussian averages the modes and blurs. (2) Pixel data is quantized to 256 levels; a discretized distribution assigns proper probability mass to each level (giving honest log-likelihoods in bits/dim) and can put finite mass on the boundary values 0 and 255, which a density cannot without truncation artifacts.

    Latent variable models

    A latent variable model says: data looks complicated because we only see half the story. First draw an unobserved cause $z$ (pose, identity, style) from a simple prior, then generate $x$ from it. The marginal $p(x)=\int p(x\mid z)p(z)\,dz$ can be rich even when both pieces are simple — but that integral is also the whole difficulty of training.

    Definition A latent variable model specifies a prior $p(z)$ and a decoder/likelihood $p_\theta(x\mid z)$; the model of the data is the marginal $$p_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x\mid z)\,p(z)\,dz.$$ Inference is the posterior $p_\theta(z\mid x)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z)/p_\theta(x)$, typically intractable — hence EM (Ch 19), variational inference (Ch 13), and VAEs (next).
    Worked example Discrete latent: $z\in\{1,2\}$ with $p(z{=}1)=0.5$; $x\mid z{=}1\sim\mathcal N(-2,1)$, $x\mid z{=}2\sim\mathcal N(2,1)$. Marginal: a bimodal mixture. At $x=1$: $p(x\mid z{=}1)=\phi(3)\approx0.0044$, $p(x\mid z{=}2)=\phi(-1)\approx0.2420$. Posterior: $p(z{=}2\mid x{=}1)=\frac{0.2420}{0.2420+0.0044}\approx0.982$ — observing $x=1$ almost certainly reveals the right-hand cause. This posterior computation is exactly what becomes intractable when $z$ is a deep continuous code.
    Drill
    1. EASY In the worked example, compute the marginal density $p(x{=}1)$.
      Solution
      $0.5\cdot0.0044+0.5\cdot0.2420=0.1232$.
    2. MED Why can a model with Gaussian $p(z)$ and Gaussian $p_\theta(x\mid z)$ with a nonlinear mean function represent non-Gaussian marginals?
      Solution
      $p_\theta(x)=\int\mathcal N(x;\mu_\theta(z),\sigma^2I)\,\mathcal N(z;0,I)dz$ is a continuous mixture of Gaussians whose centers sweep the surface $\mu_\theta(z)$ — a nonlinear image of the prior. Mixtures of Gaussians are dense in distributions (any smooth density is approximable), so a flexible $\mu_\theta$ buys arbitrarily non-Gaussian, multimodal marginals.
    3. HARD Show that for fixed $\theta$, $\log p_\theta(x)\ge\mathbb E_{q(z)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-\mathrm{KL}(q(z)\|p(z))$ for any distribution $q$, with equality iff $q=p_\theta(z\mid x)$.
      Solution
      $\log p_\theta(x)=\mathbb E_q\big[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z)}\big]+\mathrm{KL}(q\|p_\theta(z\mid x))$: expand the KL and use $p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(z\mid x)p_\theta(x)$; the $\log p_\theta(x)$ factors out of the expectation. Since KL $\ge0$ with equality iff its arguments agree, dropping it gives the bound; the remaining term rearranges to $\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$. This is the ELBO, two sections ahead.

    Autoencoders

    An autoencoder squeezes data through a bottleneck: an encoder compresses $x$ to a code $z$, a decoder reconstructs it. Trained on reconstruction error alone it is a compression/representation device, not yet a generative model — nothing constrains the code space, so decoding random codes produces junk. Its variants (denoising, VAE, VQ) fix that in different ways.

    Definition Encoder $f_\phi:\mathcal X\to\mathbb R^k$, decoder $g_\theta:\mathbb R^k\to\mathcal X$, trained to minimize reconstruction loss $$\mathcal L=\frac1n\sum_i\|x_i-g_\theta(f_\phi(x_i))\|^2,$$ with $k\ll\dim(x)$ (undercomplete) or with regularization (denoising: reconstruct $x$ from a corrupted $\tilde x$).
    Theorem (linear case = PCA) If $f,g$ are linear and the loss is squared error, the optimal rank-$k$ autoencoder spans the same subspace as the top-$k$ principal components of the data (Ch 8, Ch 19).
    Worked example Data on a line: $x=(t,2t)$ for $t\in\{-1,0,1,2\}$. A 1D linear autoencoder: encode $z=f(x)=\frac{x_1+2x_2}{5}$ (projection onto direction $(1,2)/\sqrt5$, scaled), decode $g(z)=(z,2z)$. Check $x=(2,4)$: $z=\frac{2+8}{5}=2$, reconstruction $(2,4)$ — exact, because the data truly is one-dimensional. Any point off the line reconstructs to its projection: $(1,0)\mapsto z=0.2\mapsto(0.2,0.4)$.
    Pitfall A bottleneck with too much capacity (or none, $k\ge\dim x$) can learn the identity map: perfect reconstruction, useless representation. The information constraint — narrow $k$, noise, sparsity or a prior — is what forces structure.
    Drill
    1. EASY Why is a plain autoencoder not a generative model?
      Solution
      It defines no distribution over codes: the encoder's image is an unknown, possibly thin set in $\mathbb R^k$. Sampling requires knowing which $z$'s are "valid"; decoding an arbitrary $z$ lands off the data manifold.
    2. MED In denoising autoencoding, we corrupt $\tilde x=x+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I)$, and train $g(f(\tilde x))\approx x$. What function does the optimal denoiser compute?
      Solution
      The conditional expectation $\mathbb E[x\mid\tilde x]$ — the posterior mean of the clean signal given the corrupted one (minimizer of expected squared error, Ch 11/13). This is why denoisers learn the local geometry of the data distribution — a fact that becomes the engine of diffusion models below (via Tweedie's formula).
    3. HARD For centered data with covariance $\Sigma=Q\Lambda Q^\top$, show the best linear rank-1 autoencoder achieves squared error $\sum_{i\ge2}\lambda_i$.
      Solution
      Reconstruction is a rank-1 projection $P=vv^\top$ ($\|v\|=1$ WLOG after absorbing scales). Expected error $\mathbb E\|x-Px\|^2=\mathrm{tr}((I-P)\Sigma)=\mathrm{tr}\Sigma-v^\top\Sigma v$. Maximizing $v^\top\Sigma v$ over unit $v$ gives the top eigenvector, value $\lambda_1$ (Rayleigh quotient, Ch 8). Error $=\sum_i\lambda_i-\lambda_1=\sum_{i\ge2}\lambda_i$. ∎

    Variational autoencoders

    A VAE upgrades the autoencoder into a proper latent variable model: the encoder outputs a distribution over codes (a Gaussian with predicted mean and variance), the decoder defines $p_\theta(x\mid z)$, and a KL penalty pins the code distribution to a standard normal prior. Now the code space is fully covered by the prior, so sampling $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and decoding produces genuine new data.

    Definition A VAE has prior $p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I)$, decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)$, and approximate posterior (encoder) $q_\phi(z\mid x)=\mathcal N(\mu_\phi(x),\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x)))$, trained by maximizing the ELBO $$\mathcal L(\theta,\phi;x)=\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,p(z)\big)\ \le\ \log p_\theta(x).$$
    Worked example 1D latent. For one datapoint the encoder outputs $\mu=1$, $\sigma=0.5$. KL term (Gaussian formula, next section's drill): $\mathrm{KL}=\tfrac12(\sigma^2+\mu^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)=\tfrac12(0.25+1-1-\ln0.25)=\tfrac12(0.25+1.3863)=0.818$ nats. If the expected reconstruction log-likelihood is $-1.2$ nats, the ELBO is $-1.2-0.818=-2.018$: the true $\log p_\theta(x)$ is at least $-2.018$.
    Pitfall Posterior collapse: with a powerful decoder (e.g. autoregressive), the model can ignore $z$ entirely — the KL term goes to 0, $q_\phi(z\mid x)=p(z)$ for all $x$, and the "latent" carries no information. Fixes: KL warm-up/annealing, free bits (a floor per-dimension KL), or weaker decoders.
    Where this goes The encoder-decoder-with-prior pattern returns as the latent space of latent diffusion models, and as the world-model backbone of Ch 29.
    Drill
    1. EASY To generate a new sample from a trained VAE, what do you do — and which network is not needed?
      Solution
      Draw $z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ and compute $x\sim p_\theta(x\mid z)$ (often just the decoder mean). The encoder is not used for generation — only for training and for encoding existing data.
    2. MED The ELBO can be rewritten as $\log p_\theta(x)-\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p_\theta(z\mid x))$. What does maximizing it over $\phi$ alone do?
      Solution
      With $\theta$ fixed, $\log p_\theta(x)$ is constant, so maximizing the ELBO over $\phi$ minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi\|p_\theta(z\mid x))$: variational inference — fitting the encoder to the true posterior. Training jointly does approximate EM: E-step in $\phi$, M-step in $\theta$.
    3. HARD Why do VAE image samples look blurry when $p_\theta(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(z),\sigma^2I)$ with fixed $\sigma$? Combine (i) what the decoder mean must be and (ii) posterior overlap.
      Solution
      (i) Under squared-error (Gaussian) likelihood, the optimal decoder mean is $\mathbb E[x\mid z]$. (ii) With a finite-capacity encoder, distinct sharp images $x_1,x_2$ have overlapping posteriors: the same $z$ is plausible for both. The decoder at that $z$ must output the average of the images it might explain — a pixelwise mean of sharp images is blurry. Sharper likelihood models (discretized logistics), richer posteriors, or moving generation into a latent space with a diffusion prior all attack this.

    ELBO

    The evidence lower bound is the workhorse of variational methods: since $\log p_\theta(x)$ has an intractable integral inside, we bound it from below by an expression with the integral moved outside the log, at the cost of an auxiliary distribution $q$. The bound is tight exactly when $q$ equals the true posterior, so raising the ELBO both fits the model and improves the inference network.

    Definition For any $q(z\mid x)$: $$\log p_\theta(x)=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q}\Big[\log\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q(z\mid x)}\Big]}_{\mathrm{ELBO}}+\ \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z\mid x)\,\|\,p_\theta(z\mid x)\big),$$ so $\mathrm{ELBO}\le\log p_\theta(x)$, with gap equal to the posterior KL. Equivalent form: $\mathrm{ELBO}=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]-\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)$ — reconstruction minus rate.
    Worked example Discrete check. Let $z\in\{1,2\}$, $p(z)=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$, $p(x\mid z{=}1)=0.1$, $p(x\mid z{=}2)=0.4$ for our observed $x$. True evidence: $p(x)=0.25$, $\log p(x)=-1.386$. Take $q=(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$: $\mathrm{ELBO}=\sum_zq(z)\log\frac{p(x\mid z)p(z)}{q(z)}=\tfrac12\log0.1+\tfrac12\log0.4=\tfrac12(-2.303)+\tfrac12(-0.916)=-1.610$. Gap: $-1.386-(-1.610)=0.224$ — exactly $\mathrm{KL}(q\|p(z\mid x))$ where the true posterior is $(0.2,0.8)$: $\tfrac12\log\frac{0.5}{0.2}+\tfrac12\log\frac{0.5}{0.8}=0.458-0.235=0.224$ ✓.
    Think first The ELBO splits as reconstruction minus KL-to-prior. What behavior does each term reward, and what tension results?
    Reveal
    Reconstruction wants codes that identify $x$ precisely — informative, spread-out, per-example distinct posteriors. The KL term wants every posterior to look like the same standard normal — uninformative codes. The optimum trades information in the code (the "rate", in bits) against reconstruction fidelity ("distortion"): the ELBO is a rate–distortion trade-off (Ch 16), with $\beta$-VAE making the trade-off weight explicit.
    Drill
    1. EASY When is the ELBO exactly equal to $\log p_\theta(x)$?
      Solution
      Iff $q(z\mid x)=p_\theta(z\mid x)$ — the KL gap vanishes only for the exact posterior.
    2. MED Derive the Gaussian KL used in VAEs: $\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\|\mathcal N(0,1))=\tfrac12(\sigma^2+\mu^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)$.
      Solution
      $\mathrm{KL}=\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log p]$. $\log q=-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac{(z-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$, $\log p=-\tfrac12\ln2\pi-\frac{z^2}2$. Expectations under $q$: $\mathbb E[(z-\mu)^2]=\sigma^2$, $\mathbb E[z^2]=\sigma^2+\mu^2$. So $\mathrm{KL}=-\tfrac12\ln\sigma^2-\tfrac12+\tfrac12(\sigma^2+\mu^2)=\tfrac12(\sigma^2+\mu^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)$. ∎ (Sums over dimensions for diagonal Gaussians.)
    3. HARD Show the aggregate ELBO over the dataset can be written as $-\big(\text{distortion}\big)-\big(\text{rate}\big)$ where rate $=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z))\ \ge\ I_q(x;z)$, the mutual information between data and codes under $q$.
      Solution
      Write the aggregated posterior $\bar q(z)=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}[q_\phi(z\mid x)]$. Then $\mathbb E_x\mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\|p(z))=\mathbb E_x\mathrm{KL}(q(z\mid x)\|\bar q(z))+\mathrm{KL}(\bar q\|p)=I_q(x;z)+\mathrm{KL}(\bar q\|p)\ \ge I_q(x;z)$. So the KL term charges at least the information the code carries about the input, plus a penalty for the aggregate mismatching the prior — the precise sense in which the ELBO prices bits.

    Reparameterization trick

    Training a VAE requires gradients of an expectation over $z\sim q_\phi$ — but the sampling step blocks backprop. The trick: move the randomness into a fixed noise source and make the sample a deterministic, differentiable function of $\phi$: sample $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ once, then compute $z=\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\epsilon$. Now gradients flow through $\mu$ and $\sigma$ with low variance.

    Definition If $z\sim q_\phi$ can be written $z=g_\phi(\epsilon)$ with $\epsilon\sim p(\epsilon)$ independent of $\phi$, then $$\nabla_\phi\,\mathbb E_{z\sim q_\phi}[f(z)]=\mathbb E_{\epsilon}\big[\nabla_\phi f(g_\phi(\epsilon))\big],$$ estimated by sampling $\epsilon$. For Gaussians: $g_\phi(\epsilon)=\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\epsilon$.
    Worked example $f(z)=z^2$, $q=\mathcal N(\mu,1)$. True gradient: $\frac{d}{d\mu}\mathbb E[z^2]=\frac{d}{d\mu}(\mu^2+1)=2\mu$. Reparameterized estimator with one draw $\epsilon$: $\frac{d}{d\mu}(\mu+\epsilon)^2=2(\mu+\epsilon)$ — unbiased (mean $2\mu$), variance $4$. The score-function (REINFORCE) estimator $z^2\,\frac{d}{d\mu}\log q(z)=z^2(z-\mu)$ is also unbiased but has variance $\approx4\mu^2+\dots$ growing with $\mu^2$ (at $\mu=3$: 15× worse) — the practical reason reparameterization wins when $f$ is differentiable.
    Pitfall The trick needs $f$ differentiable in $z$ and a reparameterizable $q$. Discrete latents break both; workarounds are REINFORCE (high variance), Gumbel-softmax relaxation (biased), or straight-through estimators (biased) — the tension at the heart of VQ-VAEs, next.

      Solution

    3. HARD Verify the identity $\nabla_\mu\mathbb E_{z\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)}[f(z)]=\mathbb E[f'(z)]$ (for smooth, integrable $f$), connecting reparameterization to integration by parts.
      Solution
      Reparameterize: $\mathbb E[f(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)]$; differentiate under the integral: $\mathbb E[f'(\mu+\sigma\epsilon)]=\mathbb E[f'(z)]$. Alternatively, $\nabla_\mu\int f(z)\mathcal N(z;\mu,\sigma^2)dz=\int f(z)\frac{z-\mu}{\sigma^2}\mathcal N\,dz$, and integrating by parts moves the derivative onto $f$: $\int f'(z)\mathcal N\,dz$ (boundary terms vanish). Both routes agree — the trick is a change of variables that implements integration by parts automatically.

    Vector-quantized autoencoders

    A VQ-VAE makes the latent code discrete: the encoder's output is snapped to the nearest entry of a learned codebook, so an image becomes a grid of code indices — tokens. That marries images to the autoregressive machinery of language models (train a transformer over code indices) and avoids posterior collapse, at the cost of a non-differentiable quantization step.

    Definition Codebook $\{e_1,\dots,e_K\}\subset\mathbb R^d$. Encode $z_e=f_\phi(x)$; quantize $z_q=e_{k^*}$, $k^*=\arg\min_k\|z_e-e_k\|$; decode $g_\theta(z_q)$. Loss: $$\|x-g_\theta(z_q)\|^2+\|\mathrm{sg}[z_e]-e_{k^*}\|^2+\beta\|z_e-\mathrm{sg}[e_{k^*}]\|^2,$$ with $\mathrm{sg}$ the stop-gradient; the decoder gradient passes to the encoder via the straight-through estimator ($\partial z_q/\partial z_e:=I$).
    Worked example $d=2$, codebook $e_1=(0,0)$, $e_2=(1,1)$, $e_3=(2,0)$; encoder output $z_e=(1.2,0.4)$. Distances²: to $e_1$: $1.44+0.16=1.60$; to $e_2$: $0.04+0.36=0.40$; to $e_3$: $0.64+0.16=0.80$. Quantize to $e_2=(1,1)$. Codebook loss term: $0.40$; commitment term: $\beta\cdot0.40$. The codebook entry $e_2$ moves toward $z_e$; the encoder is pulled toward $e_2$.
    Where this goes Tokenized images/audio feed autoregressive transformers (Ch 23's machinery applied beyond text); VQ codes also underpin latent diffusion variants and multimodal world models (Ch 29).

    Generative adversarial networks

    A GAN skips likelihood entirely and plays a game: a generator turns noise into fakes, a discriminator learns to tell fakes from data, and the generator learns to fool it. The discriminator is a learned, ever-improving loss function — which produces famously sharp samples and famously unstable training.

    Definition Generator $G_\theta:z\mapsto x$, $z\sim p(z)$; discriminator $D_\psi:x\mapsto[0,1]$. Minimax objective: $$\min_\theta\max_\psi\ \mathbb E_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D_\psi(x)]+\mathbb E_{z\sim p(z)}[\log(1-D_\psi(G_\theta(z)))].$$
    Theorem (Goodfellow 2014) For fixed $G$ the optimal discriminator is $D^*(x)=\frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x)+p_G(x)}$, and at $D=D^*$ the generator objective equals $2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\|p_G)-\log4$: the game minimizes Jensen–Shannon divergence (Ch 16), minimized iff $p_G=p_{\text{data}}$.
    Worked example At some $x$, $p_{\text{data}}(x)=0.3$ and $p_G(x)=0.1$: $D^*(x)=\frac{0.3}{0.4}=0.75$ — the discriminator says "75% real", exactly the posterior probability of "real" under a 50/50 prior. If the generator matches the data, $D^*\equiv\tfrac12$ everywhere: the discriminator is reduced to coin-flipping, the equilibrium signature.
    Pitfall Mode collapse: the generator can win locally by producing only a few highly convincing outputs — nothing in the objective forces coverage of all modes (contrast MLE's mass-covering KL). Symptoms: low sample diversity despite good individual quality. Mitigations: minibatch discrimination, unrolled/regularized discriminators, or switching family (diffusion).
    Drill
    1. EASY In GAN training, which network's parameters are updated to maximize the objective, and which to minimize?
      Solution
      The discriminator $\psi$ maximizes (better classification); the generator $\theta$ minimizes (better fooling). In practice they alternate gradient steps.
    2. MED Derive $D^*(x)=\frac{p_{\text{data}}}{p_{\text{data}}+p_G}$ by pointwise maximization.
      Solution
      The objective is $\int[p_{\text{data}}(x)\log D(x)+p_G(x)\log(1-D(x))]dx$; maximize the integrand at each $x$: $\frac{d}{dD}[a\log D+b\log(1-D)]=\frac aD-\frac{b}{1-D}=0\Rightarrow D=\frac{a}{a+b}$ with $a=p_{\text{data}}(x)$, $b=p_G(x)$. Second derivative negative, so a maximum. ∎
    3. HARD Why does the original generator loss $\log(1-D(G(z)))$ saturate early in training, and what is the standard "non-saturating" fix?
      Solution
      Early on, fakes are obvious: $D(G(z))\approx0$, and $\frac{d}{du}\log(1-u)=-\frac{1}{1-u}\approx-1$ — bounded, but the discriminator's confidence makes $D$'s output sit in a flat region of its own sigmoid, so gradients through $D$ to $G$ are tiny: $\nabla\log(1-D)= -\frac{\nabla D}{1-D}\approx-\nabla D$, and $\nabla D\approx0$ in the saturated region. The fix: maximize $\log D(G(z))$ instead — same fixed points, but gradient $\frac{\nabla D}{D}$ is amplified (divided by small $D$) exactly when fakes are bad, giving strong early learning signal.

    Energy-based models

    An energy-based model scores every configuration with an unnormalized "energy": low energy = plausible. The density is the Boltzmann form $e^{-E}/Z$ — maximally flexible, since any function can be an energy — but the normalizer $Z$ is an integral over all of data space, and it poisons both likelihood evaluation and sampling.

    Definition $$p_\theta(x)=\frac{e^{-E_\theta(x)}}{Z(\theta)},\qquad Z(\theta)=\int e^{-E_\theta(x)}dx.$$ The log-likelihood gradient is $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=-\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x)+\mathbb E_{x'\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta E_\theta(x')],$$ — push energy down on data, up on model samples ("contrastive divergence" when samples come from short MCMC chains).
    Worked example Two states $\{a,b\}$ with $E(a)=0$, $E(b)=1$. $Z=e^0+e^{-1}=1.3679$; $p(a)=1/1.3679\approx0.731$, $p(b)=0.269$. Note $p(a)/p(b)=e^{E(b)-E(a)}=e\approx2.718$: only energy differences matter — adding a constant to all energies cancels in $Z$.
    Where this goes The gradient of the log-density $\nabla_x\log p(x)=-\nabla_xE(x)$ needs no $Z$ — that observation is the gateway to score matching and diffusion, next. Boltzmann distributions also return in Ch 12 (Langevin) and Ch 25 (softmax policies).
    Drill
    1. EASY Why does $\nabla_x\log p_\theta(x)$ not involve $Z(\theta)$?
      Solution
      $\log p=-E_\theta(x)-\log Z(\theta)$, and $Z$ does not depend on $x$: $\nabla_x\log p=-\nabla_xE_\theta(x)$.
    2. MED Derive the two-term likelihood gradient in the definition.
      Solution
      $\nabla_\theta\log p=-\nabla_\theta E(x)-\nabla_\theta\log Z$. And $\nabla_\theta\log Z=\frac1Z\int e^{-E(x')}(-\nabla_\theta E(x'))dx'=-\mathbb E_{p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta E(x')]$. Substituting gives the data term minus (negative) model term. The intractable part is the model expectation — hence MCMC approximations.
    3. HARD A Gibbs/Boltzmann distribution at temperature $T$ is $p_T(x)\propto e^{-E(x)/T}$. Show that as $T\to0$, $p_T$ concentrates on global minima of $E$, and compute $p_T(a),p_T(b)$ for the worked example at $T=0.2$.
      Solution
      $p_T(x)/p_T(x^*)=e^{-(E(x)-E(x^*))/T}\to0$ for any $x$ with $E(x)>E(x^*)=\min E$; mass concentrates uniformly on the argmin set. At $T=0.2$: ratio $=e^{-1/0.2}=e^{-5}\approx0.0067$; $p(a)=1/(1+0.0067)\approx0.9933$, $p(b)\approx0.0067$.     Normalizing flows

    A normalizing flow generates by pushing simple noise through an invertible network, and — because it's invertible — can compute the exact density of any point by running backward and tracking how volumes stretched. Exact likelihoods and exact sampling in one model; the price is architectural: every layer must be invertible with a tractable Jacobian determinant.

    Definition Let $x=f_\theta(z)$, $z\sim p_Z$ with $f_\theta$ a diffeomorphism. Change of variables (Ch 11): $$\log p_X(x)=\log p_Z(f_\theta^{-1}(x))+\log\Big|\det\frac{\partial f_\theta^{-1}}{\partial x}\Big|.$$ Flows compose layers whose Jacobians are triangular (coupling layers, autoregressive flows) so the log-determinant is a sum of diagonal terms.
    Worked example 1D affine flow $x=f(z)=2z+1$, $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$. Inverse $z=(x-1)/2$, $|dz/dx|=\tfrac12$. Density at $x=3$: $z=1$, $p_X(3)=\phi(1)\cdot\tfrac12=0.2420\cdot0.5=0.1210$. Check: $X\sim\mathcal N(1,4)$, so $p_X(3)=\frac{1}{2}\phi\big(\frac{3-1}{2}\big)=\tfrac12\phi(1)$ ✓ — the flow computed the exact Gaussian density by bookkeeping the stretch factor.

    Worked example (coupling layer) Split $z=(z_1,z_2)$; keep $x_1=z_1$, transform $x_2=z_2\,e^{s(z_1)}+t(z_1)$ with arbitrary networks $s,t$.     Score functions

    The score is the gradient of the log-density with respect to the data point: a vector field over data space in which every arrow points "uphill toward higher probability". Knowing the score everywhere is as good as knowing the distribution — you can sample by following the arrows with noise (Langevin dynamics, Ch 12) — and, crucially, the score never involves the normalizing constant.

    Definition The (Stein) score of a density $p$ is $$s(x)=\nabla_x\log p(x).$$ For an EBM, $s(x)=-\nabla_xE(x)$. Langevin sampling: $x_{k+1}=x_k+\frac{\eta}{2}s(x_k)+\sqrt{\eta}\,\epsilon_k$, $\epsilon_k\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, converges to $p$ as $\eta\to0$, steps $\to\infty$.
    Explore

    The arrows show the score field of a two-Gaussian mixture; press Run to release Langevin particles and watch them settle into the modes. Slide the mode separation.

    Worked example Gaussian $p=\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)$: $\log p=-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}+c$, so $s(x)=\frac{\mu-x}{\sigma^2}$ — an arrow pointing at the mean, stronger farther away. For the mixture $\tfrac12\mathcal N(-2,1)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(2,1)$ at $x=0$: by symmetry the two pulls cancel, $s(0)=0$ — a saddle of the log-density between modes.

    1. EASY Compute the score of $\mathcal N(1,4)$ at $x=3$.
      Solution
      $s(3)=\frac{1-3}{4}=-0.5$ — pointing back toward the mean.
    2. MED Show the score of a mixture $p=\sum_k\pi_kp_k$ is the posterior-weighted average of component scores: $s(x)=\sum_kw_k(x)s_k(x)$ with $w_k(x)=\frac{\pi_kp_k(x)}{p(x)}$.

    3. HARD Why is a pure gradient ascent on $\log p$ (Langevin without noise) a bad sampler, and what exactly does the $\sqrt\eta\,\epsilon$ term fix?
      Solution
      Noiseless ascent converges to the nearest mode and stays: the limiting "distribution" is point masses at local maxima weighted by basin of attraction — not $p$ (it ignores each mode's width/mass and has zero entropy). The injected noise makes the process a discretized SDE whose stationary distribution is $p$ itself (Fokker–Planck, Ch 12): the noise supplies the entropy that lets samples spread through each mode in proportion to its probability, and (over long times) hop between modes.

    Score matching

    If we can't compute $Z$, let's not fit densities — let's fit scores. Score matching trains a network $s_\theta(x)$ to equal $\nabla_x\log p_{\text{data}}(x)$ by minimizing their squared difference. The magic: an integration-by-parts identity removes the unknown data score from the objective, leaving only computable terms.

    Definition Explicit objective: $J(\theta)=\tfrac12\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}\|s_\theta(x)-\nabla_x\log p_{\text{data}}(x)\|^2$. Hyvärinen's identity: up to a $\theta$-free constant, $$J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{p_{\text{data}}}\Big[\tfrac12\|s_\theta(x)\|^2+\nabla_x\!\cdot\!s_\theta(x)\Big],$$ where $\nabla\!\cdot\!s_\theta=\sum_i\partial s_{\theta,i}/\partial x_i$ (trace of the Jacobian) — no $p_{\text{data}}$ gradients, no $Z$.
    Worked example Fit a linear score $s_\theta(x)=-\theta x$ (i.e. a centered Gaussian with precision $\theta$) to data with $\mathbb E[x^2]=4$ (1D). Objective: $\mathbb E[\tfrac12\theta^2x^2-\theta]=\tfrac12\theta^2\cdot4-\theta$ (since $\partial_x(-\theta x)=-\theta$). Minimize: $4\theta-1=0\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac14$ — precision $=1/\text{variance}$, exactly the Gaussian MLE. Score matching recovered it without ever normalizing.
    Pitfall The divergence term needs $\sum_i\partial s_i/\partial x_i$ — a trace of a Jacobian, costing $d$ backprops in $d$ dimensions. For images ($d\sim10^5$) that's hopeless. Practical routes: sliced score matching (random-projection Hutchinson estimates of the trace) or denoising score matching, next — which is what everyone actually uses.
    Drill
    1. EASY Why does score matching sidestep the normalizing constant?
      Solution
      It matches $\nabla_x\log p$, and $\log Z$ has zero $x$-gradient: scores of $e^{-E}/Z$ and of unnormalized $e^{-E}$ are identical.
    2. MED Sketch the integration-by-parts step of Hyvärinen's identity in 1D.
      Solution
      Expand $J=\tfrac12\mathbb E[s_\theta^2]-\mathbb E[s_\theta\,(\log p)']+c$. The cross term: $\int p\,s_\theta\,\frac{p'}{p}dx=\int s_\theta\,p'\,dx=[s_\theta p]_{-\infty}^{\infty}-\int s_\theta'\,p\,dx=-\mathbb E[s_\theta'(x)]$ (boundary terms vanish for decaying $p$). So $J=\mathbb E[\tfrac12s_\theta^2+s_\theta']+c$. ∎
    3. HARD Fit $s_\theta(x)=-\theta(x-m)$ with both $\theta,m$ free to data with mean $1$ and variance $4$, using the Hyvärinen objective.
      Solution
      $J=\mathbb E[\tfrac12\theta^2(x-m)^2-\theta]$. $\partial J/\partial m=-\theta^2\mathbb E[x-m]=0\Rightarrow m=\mathbb E[x]=1$. Then $\mathbb E[(x-m)^2]=\mathrm{Var}=4$ and $\partial J/\partial\theta=4\theta-1=0\Rightarrow\theta=\tfrac14$. Recovered $\mathcal N(1,4)$: score matching consistently estimates exponential-family parameters.

    Denoising score matching

    Denoising score matching replaces the expensive divergence term with a beautifully simple target: corrupt the data with Gaussian noise, and train the network to point from the noisy point back toward the clean one. The optimal solution is exactly the score of the noised data distribution — and "predict the noise" becomes the training objective of every diffusion model.

    Definition Let $\tilde x=x+\sigma\epsilon$, $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$, and $p_\sigma$ be the density of $\tilde x$. Vincent's theorem: minimizing $$\mathbb E_{x\sim p_{\text{data}},\,\epsilon}\Big\|s_\theta(\tilde x)-\underbrace{\Big(-\frac{\epsilon}{\sigma}\Big)}_{\nabla_{\tilde x}\log q(\tilde x\mid x)}\Big\|^2$$ yields $s_\theta(\tilde x)=\nabla_{\tilde x}\log p_\sigma(\tilde x)$ — the true score of the smoothed distribution.
    Worked example Data concentrated at the single point $x_0=2$ (a delta), noise $\sigma=1$. Then $p_\sigma=\mathcal N(2,1)$, whose true score at $\tilde x=3$ is $\frac{2-3}{1}=-1$. The DSM target for a sample that landed at $\tilde x=3$ (so $\epsilon=1$): $-\epsilon/\sigma=-1$ ✓ — pointing from the noisy point straight back at the data. With many data points, the optimal network outputs the posterior-averaged direction $\mathbb E[x\mid\tilde x]$-ward: $s(\tilde x)=\frac{\mathbb E[x\mid\tilde x]-\tilde x}{\sigma^2}$ (Tweedie's formula).
    Where this goes Train this at many noise scales and you have a diffusion model's denoiser; Tweedie's formula is the bridge between "noise prediction" and "score" used constantly in Ch 22's remaining sections.

    Diffusion models

    A diffusion model destroys data slowly and learns to rebuild it: the forward process adds a little Gaussian noise at each of many steps until only noise remains; a network learns to undo one step at a time; generation starts from pure noise and runs the learned denoising chain backward. Each step is a small, easy regression problem — which is why training is stable where GANs are fragile.

    Definition Forward (fixed): $q(x_t\mid x_{t-1})=\mathcal N(\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_tI)$ for $t=1..T$ with small variances $\beta_t$. Closed form: $$q(x_t\mid x_0)=\mathcal N(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)I),\qquad \bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s),$$ so $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$. Reverse (learned): $p_\theta(x_{t-1}\mid x_t)=\mathcal N(\mu_\theta(x_t,t),\tilde\beta_tI)$, trained so the reverse chain inverts the forward one.

    Slide $t$ to watch a bimodal data distribution dissolve into a Gaussian under the forward process — then imagine running the movie backward: that is generation.

    Worked example Linear schedule reaching $\bar\alpha_t=0.64$ at some $t$. Then $x_t=0.8\,x_0+0.6\,\epsilon$ (since $\sqrt{0.64}=0.8$, $\sqrt{0.36}=0.6$). For a data point $x_0=2$ and noise draw $\epsilon=-1$: $x_t=1.6-0.6=1.0$. Signal-to-noise ratio at this $t$: $\frac{\bar\alpha_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}=\frac{0.64}{0.36}\approx1.78$; by $\bar\alpha=0.01$ the SNR is $\approx0.01$ — the image is gone.

    DDPM

    DDPM is the concrete recipe that made diffusion work: parameterize the reverse mean by predicting the noise $\epsilon$ that was added, train with plain squared error on that prediction at random noise levels, and sample by iteratively subtracting predicted noise with a bit of fresh noise re-injected. The exotic-looking ELBO of the diffusion chain collapses to "guess the noise".

    Definition Training loss (simplified ELBO): $$\mathcal L_{\text{simple}}=\mathbb E_{x_0,\epsilon,t}\big\|\epsilon-\epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2.$$ Sampling step: $$x_{t-1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t-\frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t,t)\Big)+\sqrt{\tilde\beta_t}\,z,\quad z\sim\mathcal N(0,I)\ (z=0\text{ at }t=1).$$
    Worked example 1D, one step with $\alpha_t=0.99$ ($\beta_t=0.01$), $\bar\alpha_t=0.81$, current $x_t=1.2$, network predicts $\epsilon_\theta=0.9$. Mean: $\frac{1}{\sqrt{0.99}}\big(1.2-\frac{0.01}{\sqrt{0.19}}\cdot0.9\big)=\frac{1}{0.99499}(1.2-\frac{0.009}{0.43589})=\frac{1.2-0.020648}{0.99499}\approx\frac{1.17935}{0.99499}\approx1.1853$. Mixing conventions (predicting $x_0$, $\epsilon$, or "v") with the wrong coefficients is the most common diffusion implementation bug; each is a linear reparameterization of the same target, with different loss weightings across $t$.

      Solution
      The reverse process is a distribution $\mathcal N(\tilde\mu,\tilde\beta_tI)$: its variance is part of matching the forward chain's posterior.     Score-based SDE view of diffusion

    Zoom out from discrete steps and diffusion becomes a stochastic differential equation (Ch 12): the forward SDE drifts data toward noise; a classical result of Anderson says the time-reversed process is again an SDE whose drift needs exactly one unknown — the score of the noised marginal at each time. Learn the score, plug it in, integrate backward: that is sampling. The same marginals can even be traversed by a deterministic ODE.

    Definition Forward SDE: $dx=f(x,t)\,dt+g(t)\,dW$ (e.g. VP-SDE $dx=-\tfrac12\beta(t)x\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}dW$, the continuous limit of DDPM). Reverse-time SDE: $$dx=\big[f(x,t)-g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]dt+g(t)\,d\bar W,$$ run from $t=T$ to $0$. Probability-flow ODE with identical marginals: $dx=\big[f-\tfrac12g^2\nabla_x\log p_t\big]dt$.
    Worked example Ornstein–Uhlenbeck forward: $dx=-x\,dt+\sqrt2\,dW$ ($f=-x$, $g=\sqrt2$), stationary at $\mathcal N(0,1)$. Suppose the data at $t$ is $p_t=\mathcal N(m_t,1)$ (unit variance for simplicity); its score is $s(x)=m_t-x$. Reverse drift: $f-g^2s=-x-2(m_t-x)=x-2m_t$: for $x$ below $m_t$... evaluate at $x=m_t$: drift $=-m_t$ pushing along the reversed mean path; at $x=m_t+1$: drift $=1-m_t$ — relative to the mean the reverse drift is $+(x-m_t)\cdot1$ pointing away in forward time, i.e. contracting toward the data as reverse time advances. The learned score is the only data-dependent ingredient.
    Where this goes Ch 12's reverse-time SDEs supply the theorem; the ODE view underlies fast samplers (DDIM, DPM-Solver) and exact likelihood computation via the change-of-variables along the flow.
    Drill
    1. EASY In the reverse SDE, which single object must be learned from data?
      Solution
      The time-indexed score $\nabla_x\log p_t(x)$ — everything else ($f$, $g$) was chosen by us in the forward process.
    2. MED Why does the probability-flow ODE enable exact likelihoods for a diffusion model?
      Solution
      The ODE is a deterministic, invertible flow between $p_0$ (data) and $p_T$ (Gaussian) with the same marginals as the SDE. Integrate the instantaneous change-of-variables (continuous normalizing flow): $\log p_0(x_0)=\log p_T(x_T)+\int_0^T\nabla\!\cdot\!\big[f-\tfrac12g^2s_\theta\big]dt$ along the trajectory — an exact density, computed with an ODE solver plus (Hutchinson-estimated) divergence.
    3. HARD For the VP-SDE $dx=-\tfrac12\beta(t)x\,dt+\sqrt{\beta(t)}dW$ with constant $\beta$, derive the marginal of $x_t$ given $x_0$ and match it to the DDPM closed form.
      Solution
      Linear SDE: mean obeys $\dot m=-\tfrac12\beta m$, so $m_t=x_0e^{-\beta t/2}$; variance obeys $\dot v=-\beta v+\beta$, so $v_t=1-e^{-\beta t}$ (starting at 0). Thus $x_t\sim\mathcal N(x_0e^{-\beta t/2},\,1-e^{-\beta t})$ — exactly $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}=e^{-\beta t/2}$, $1-\bar\alpha_t=1-e^{-\beta t}$: DDPM's $\bar\alpha_t$ is the continuous decay $e^{-\int_0^t\beta(s)ds}$, and the discrete chain is its Euler discretization.

    Classifier-free guidance

    Conditional diffusion ("an image of a cat") often follows the prompt too weakly. Classifier-free guidance trains one network to denoise both with and without the condition (randomly dropping it during training), then at sampling time extrapolates past the conditional prediction, amplifying exactly the direction in which the condition changes the score. One knob, $w$, trades diversity for prompt-fidelity.

    Definition With conditional and unconditional noise predictions $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,c)$ and $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)$, guided prediction: $$\tilde\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)+w\big(\epsilon_\theta(x_t,c)-\epsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)\big),$$ $w=1$: plain conditional; $w>1$: over-emphasize the condition. Score view: $\tilde s=(1-w)s(x)+w\,s(x\mid c)$, sampling from a sharpened $p(x\mid c)\propto p(x)\,p(c\mid x)^{w}$ (up to normalization).
    Explore

    Two classes of a 1D mixture. Slide the guidance weight $w$ and watch the effective sampling density sharpen onto the conditioned class — at large $w$ it narrows past the true conditional (diversity loss).

    Worked example At some $x_t$, $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)=0.50$ and $\epsilon_\theta(x_t,c)=0.30$. With $w=3$: $\tilde\epsilon=0.50+3(0.30-0.50)=0.50-0.60=-0.10$ — beyond the conditional prediction, in the direction the condition pulls. The denoising step then moves $x_t$ more aggressively toward the class-consistent region.
    Pitfall Large $w$ (say $>7$ for images) causes saturation and artifacts: the extrapolated score is no valid distribution's score, samples drift off-manifold, and diversity collapses to class archetypes. Practical systems pair high $w$ with tricks (dynamic thresholding, rescaling).
    Drill
    1. EASY What does $w=0$ produce?
      Solution
      $\tilde\epsilon=\epsilon_\theta(x_t,\varnothing)$: unconditional generation — the prompt is ignored.
    2. MED Show the score form: $\tilde s=s(x)+w[s(x\mid c)-s(x)]$ corresponds to the density $p(x)p(c\mid x)^w$ up to normalization.
      Solution
      Bayes: $s(x\mid c)=\nabla\log p(x)+\nabla\log p(c\mid x)$. So $\tilde s=s(x)+w\nabla\log p(c\mid x)=\nabla\log\big[p(x)p(c\mid x)^w\big]$ — a classifier-tilted distribution, sharpened by exponent $w$. (Classifier guidance uses an actual classifier's gradient; classifier-free obtains the same difference from the two denoisers.)
    3. HARD Both class conditionals are $\mathcal N(\pm\mu,1)$ with equal priors. Under guidance toward class $+$ with weight $w$, what is the effective sampling distribution in the Gaussian approximation near the $+$ mode, and what happens to its variance as $w$ grows?
      Solution
      $p(x)p(+\mid x)^w$ with $p(+\mid x)=\sigma(2\mu x)$ (logistic in $x$). Near the $+$ mode, take the tilted log-density $\log p(x\mid +)+ (w-1)\log p(+\mid x)$: the extra term adds $\approx(w-1)\cdot$ (positive slope, negative curvature $-4\mu^2\sigma(1-\sigma)$ region) around the decision boundary — pushing the mode right and, since the guidance term's curvature is negative where the classifier is uncertain, narrowing the distribution. In the crude two-Gaussian score-mixture view $\tilde s=(1-w)s_{\text{mix}}+w\,s_+$, deep in the $+$ region $s_{\text{mix}}\approx s_+$ so little changes, but between the modes the pull toward $+$ scales with $w$: mass is evacuated from the boundary region, variance shrinks and samples concentrate on the class archetype — the observed diversity loss.

    Flow matching

    Flow matching asks: instead of learning scores and integrating an SDE, why not directly learn the velocity field of a deterministic flow that transports noise to data? Pick, for every data point, a simple path from a noise sample to it (a straight line works); train a network to regress the path's velocity at random times. Sampling is then just ODE integration of the learned field.

    Definition Choose a conditional path $x_t=\psi_t(x_0,x_1)$ from noise $x_0\sim\mathcal N(0,I)$ to data $x_1$, with velocity $u_t(x_t\mid x_1)=\partial_t\psi_t$. Conditional flow matching loss: $$\mathcal L=\mathbb E_{t,\,x_0,\,x_1}\big\|v_\theta(x_t,t)-u_t(x_t\mid x_1)\big\|^2,$$ whose minimizer is the marginal velocity field generating $p_t$; sampling integrates $\dot x=v_\theta(x,t)$ from $t=0$ to 1.
    Worked example Linear path: $x_t=(1-t)x_0+t\,x_1$, so $u_t=x_1-x_0$ — constant along each path. Take $x_0=-1$ (noise draw), $x_1=3$ (data): at $t=0.5$, $x_{0.5}=1$ and the regression target at $(x{=}1,t{=}0.5)$ is $u=4$. Another pair passing nearby contributes its own target; the network learns the average velocity of all paths through each $(x,t)$ — which is exactly the marginal field.
    Where this goes Flow matching generalizes the probability-flow ODE view of diffusion (a specific curved path choice) and is the training recipe behind recent image/video generators; rectified flow (next) is the straight-path special case with a self-straightening trick.
    Drill
    1. EASY For the linear path with $x_0=2$, $x_1=0$, what are $x_{0.25}$ and the velocity target?
      Solution
      $x_{0.25}=0.75\cdot2+0.25\cdot0=1.5$; $u=x_1-x_0=-2$ (constant in $t$).
    2. MED Why is regressing the conditional velocity (per pair) sufficient to learn the marginal field?
      Solution
      Squared-error regression's minimizer is the conditional expectation: $v^*(x,t)=\mathbb E[u_t(x_t\mid x_1)\mid x_t=x]$. The continuity equation is linear in velocity, so the expectation of conditional velocities over paths through $(x,t)$ is precisely the velocity field transporting the marginal $p_t$ — averaging valid per-sample transports yields a valid transport of the mixture.
    3. HARD Compare one flow-matching sampling step to one DDPM step on three axes: stochasticity, number of function evaluations to good quality, and what the network output represents.
      Solution
      Stochasticity: flow matching integrates a deterministic ODE (same noise draw → same image); DDPM's kernel injects noise each step. Function evaluations: straight-ish learned fields integrate accurately in few steps (tens, or even 1–4 after rectification/distillation) versus DDPM's hundreds–1000 (its SDE discretization needs small steps). Output semantics: $v_\theta$ is a velocity (displacement per unit time toward data); $\epsilon_\theta$ is a noise estimate, convertible to a score. The families are linked: the probability-flow ODE of a diffusion is one particular flow-matching field.

    Rectified flow

    Rectified flow takes flow matching's straight-line paths seriously: train on straight interpolations, then reflow — regenerate pairs by running your own model (coupling each noise sample to the data point it produces) and retrain on those. Each round straightens the learned trajectories further; near-straight trajectories can be integrated in very few steps, even one.

    Definition Rectified flow trains $v_\theta$ on linear paths $x_t=(1-t)x_0+tx_1$ with loss $\mathbb E\|v_\theta(x_t,t)-(x_1-x_0)\|^2$, where initially $(x_0,x_1)$ are independent noise/data draws. The reflow operation replaces the coupling with $(x_0,\hat x_1)$ where $\hat x_1=\mathrm{ODE}_{v_\theta}(x_0)$, and retrains; each reflow provably does not increase (and typically decreases) transport cost and straightens paths.
    Worked example 1D data at $\{-1,+1\}$ (equal mass), noise $\mathcal N(0,1)$. With independent coupling, a noise point near $0$ is paired half the time with $-1$ and half with $+1$: the marginal velocity at $(x{\approx}0,t{\approx}0)$ averages to $\approx0$ and trajectories must curve later to commit. After one reflow, each noise point is paired with the mode its own trajectory reached — negative noise with $-1$, positive with $+1$ (approximately): targets through any point now agree, the field near 0 points decisively, and paths become nearly straight lines from $x_0<0$ to $-1$, $x_0>0$ to $+1$. A one-step Euler jump $x_1\approx x_0+v(x_0,0)$ then lands close to the modes.
    Think first Why can't the first-round model already have straight trajectories, if it was trained on straight paths?
    Reveal
    Because paths from different pairs cross: at a crossing point the network must output one velocity — the average — so the realized ODE trajectories bend even though every training path was straight. Straightness of targets ≠ straightness of the marginal flow. Reflow eliminates crossings by re-pairing noise with its own destinations (a deterministic coupling can't cross itself... more precisely, ODE trajectories of a Lipschitz field never cross in $(x,t)$), so targets through each point agree and the field's trajectories straighten.
    Drill
    1. EASY Why do straight trajectories permit few-step sampling?
      Solution
      Euler integration is exact on straight lines: $x_1=x_0+1\cdot v$ in a single step; curvature is what forces small steps.
    2. MED In the worked example before reflow, what is the marginal velocity at exactly $x=0$, $t=0$, and why?
      Solution
      Targets are $x_1-x_0=\pm1-0$, each with probability $\tfrac12$ (both modes equally likely partners): conditional expectation $=0$. The model must "stall" at the symmetric point — the crossing problem in miniature.
    3. HARD Show that reflow cannot increase the expected transport cost $\mathbb E\|x_1-x_0\|^2$ of the coupling.
      Solution
      For the ODE coupling, the path from $x_0$ to $\hat x_1$ has length $\int_0^1\|v(x_t,t)\|dt$, and by Cauchy–Schwarz/Jensen, $\|\hat x_1-x_0\|^2=\|\int v\,dt\|^2\le\int\|v\|^2dt$. Meanwhile the training objective ensures $\mathbb E\int\|v\|^2dt\le\mathbb E\int\|u_t\|^2dt=\mathbb E\|x_1-x_0\|^2$ of the previous coupling (the marginal field is an $L^2$ projection — averaging — of conditional velocities, and averaging cannot increase second moments). Chaining: new cost $\le$ old cost, with equality only if paths never crossed. ∎ (This is Liu et al.'s straightening lemma in sketch.)

    Likelihood-free generation

    Some models never define a density at all: they only know how to sample (an implicit model, like a GAN generator or a simulator). Training and evaluating such models needs likelihood-free principles — adversarial discriminators, moment matching between sample sets, or simulation-based inference — all ways of comparing two piles of samples without ever writing $p(x)$.

    Definition An implicit generative model is a sampler $x=G_\theta(z)$, $z\sim p(z)$, with no tractable $p_\theta(x)$. Likelihood-free training minimizes a discrepancy estimable from samples alone, e.g. maximum mean discrepancy $$\mathrm{MMD}^2(p,q)=\mathbb E_{x,x'\sim p}[k(x,x')]-2\,\mathbb E_{x\sim p,y\sim q}[k(x,y)]+\mathbb E_{y,y'\sim q}[k(y,y')]$$ for a kernel $k$ (Ch 19), or an adversarial divergence (GAN), or a scoring-rule/simulation-based posterior.
    Worked example MMD with linear kernel $k(x,y)=xy$ on samples $P=\{1,3\}$, $Q=\{0,2\}$. $\mathbb E_{P}[xx']=\mathrm{mean}\{1,3,3,9\}=4$; $\mathbb E_{PQ}[xy]=\mathrm{mean}\{0,2,0,6\}=2$; $\mathbb E_Q[yy']=\mathrm{mean}\{0,0,0,4\}=1$. $\mathrm{MMD}^2=4-4+1=1=(\bar P-\bar Q)^2=(2-1)^2$ ✓ — the linear kernel compares means only; richer kernels (RBF) compare all moments.
    Pitfall "The model gave my test point high quality" is not a likelihood statement: implicit models cannot be compared by held-out log-likelihood at all, and sample-quality metrics can be gamed (a model that memorizes the training set has superb samples). Always pair fidelity metrics with diversity/coverage and memorization checks.
    Drill
    1. EASY Name two model families in this chapter with tractable likelihoods and two without.
      Solution
      Tractable: autoregressive models, normalizing flows (VAEs give a bound; diffusion gives a bound or ODE-exact value). Not tractable: GANs, generic simulators (implicit models).
    2. MED Show $\mathrm{MMD}^2\ge0$ and identify when it is 0 for a characteristic kernel.
      Solution
      $\mathrm{MMD}^2=\|\mu_p-\mu_q\|_{\mathcal H}^2$ where $\mu_p=\mathbb E[k(x,\cdot)]$ is the kernel mean embedding — expanding the squared RKHS norm gives exactly the three-term formula, hence $\ge0$. For characteristic kernels (e.g. RBF), the embedding is injective: $\mathrm{MMD}=0\iff p=q$.
    3. HARD Why does the GAN discriminator count as a "learned two-sample test", and what failure mode follows when the discriminator is too weak?
      Solution
      $D$ is trained to distinguish samples of $p_{\text{data}}$ from $p_G$ — literally a classifier-based two-sample test; its achievable accuracy is a divergence estimate (optimal $D$'s loss encodes JSD). If $D$'s capacity is too low, distributions that differ in features $D$ cannot represent are "equal" to the test: the generator can win while wrong in every way $D$ is blind to (e.g. global counts, long-range structure). The training signal is only as good as the test — which is also why evaluation metrics based on weak features (next section) inherit blind spots.

    Evaluating generative models

    How do you grade a machine for making things? Three axes: fidelity (are individual samples good?), diversity/coverage (is the whole distribution represented?), and generalization (is it new, or memorized?). No single number captures all three; the standard tools each formalize a slice — log-likelihood, FID-style embedding distances, precision/recall for distributions, and human evaluation.

    Definition Fréchet Inception Distance: embed real and generated samples with a fixed feature network; fit Gaussians $(\mu_r,\Sigma_r)$, $(\mu_g,\Sigma_g)$; then $$\mathrm{FID}=\|\mu_r-\mu_g\|^2+\mathrm{tr}\big(\Sigma_r+\Sigma_g-2(\Sigma_r\Sigma_g)^{1/2}\big)$$ — the Fréchet (Wasserstein-2) distance between the two Gaussians. Lower is better. Complementary: log-likelihood in bits/dim (where available), and distributional precision (fraction of generated samples near the real manifold) vs recall (fraction of real modes covered).
    Worked example 1D embeddings: real $(\mu_r,\sigma_r^2)=(0,1)$, generated $(\mu_g,\sigma_g^2)=(0.5,0.64)$. $\mathrm{FID}=(0.5)^2+\big(1+0.64-2\sqrt{1\cdot0.64}\big)=0.25+(1.64-1.6)=0.25+0.04=0.29$. A mode-collapsed model with perfect mean but $\sigma_g^2=0.09$: $0+(1.09-0.6)=0.49$ — worse, because FID's trace term punishes missing variance (diversity), not just wrong means.
    Model A memorizes the training set and emits its images verbatim. What do FID (against a held-out set drawn from the same distribution) and a memorization check report?
    Training data has the true distribution's statistics, so embedded moments match and FID is excellent — FID cannot detect memorization. Nearest-neighbor distance from each sample to the training set exposes the copying; generalization must be tested separately from fidelity.
    Drill
    1. EASY Which failure does high precision + low recall indicate?
      Solution
      Mode collapse: samples are individually realistic (near the data manifold) but cover only part of it — classic GAN pathology.
    2. MED Give one concrete reason likelihood and sample quality can disagree in both directions.
      Solution
      Great likelihood, poor samples: likelihood is dominated by imperceptible-noise modeling (bits/dim rewards fine texture statistics), and a model can spread mass thinly over good regions it can't actually render sharply. Poor likelihood, great samples: a mode-collapsed sharp model assigns $\approx0$ density to most real data (infinite penalty) while producing lovely images from the modes it kept. The two metrics measure different projections of the mismatch.
    3. HARD Compute FID for 1D embeddings with real $(0,1)$ and generated $(0,4)$, and explain why FID's Gaussian assumption can mislead for multimodal embedding distributions.
      Solution
      $\mathrm{FID}=0+(1+4-2\cdot\sqrt4)=5-4=1$. FID sees only the first two moments: a bimodal generated distribution with matching mean/covariance scores 0 against a unimodal real one despite being visibly different; conversely two identical-up-to-skew distributions can score badly. Remedies: kernel-based distances (KID/MMD with characteristic kernels) or precision/recall curves that examine the actual supports.

    1. MED One VAE step, end to end. A 1D VAE has decoder $p_\theta(x\mid z)=\mathcal N(2z,1)$ and encoder output $\mu=0.6$, $\sigma=0.8$ for a data point $x=1$. (a) Reparameterize with $\epsilon=0.5$ and compute $z$. (b) Compute the reconstruction log-likelihood $\log p_\theta(x{=}1\mid z)$. (c) Compute the KL term and the single-sample ELBO estimate.
      Solution
      (a) $z=0.6+0.8\cdot0.5=1.0$. (b) $\log\mathcal N(1;2\cdot1,1)=-\tfrac12\ln2\pi-\tfrac12(1-2)^2=-0.9189-0.5=-1.4189$. (c) $\mathrm{KL}=\tfrac12(\sigma^2+\mu^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)=\tfrac12(0.64+0.36-1-\ln0.64)=\tfrac12(0+0.4463)=0.2231$. ELBO estimate $=-1.4189-0.2231=-1.6420$ nats.
    2. HARD Score, Tweedie, and one denoising step agree. Data: $x_0\sim\mathcal N(1,\,0.25)$ (a Gaussian "dataset"). Forward noise to $\bar\alpha=0.64$: $x_t=0.8x_0+0.6\epsilon$. (a) Find the exact marginal of $x_t$. (b) Compute the exact score at $x_t=0.2$. (c) Use Tweedie to get $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t{=}0.2]$ — careful: apply it in the scaled variable. (d) Verify with the optimal $\epsilon$-prediction and $\hat x_0=(x_t-0.6\hat\epsilon)/0.8$.
      Solution
      (a) $x_t=0.8x_0+0.6\epsilon$: mean $0.8\cdot1=0.8$; variance $0.64\cdot0.25+0.36=0.16+0.36=0.52$. $x_t\sim\mathcal N(0.8,0.52)$. (b) $s(0.2)=\frac{0.8-0.2}{0.52}=1.1538$. (c) Tweedie for $x_t=0.8x_0+0.6\epsilon$: work with $y=x_t$, prior on $m=0.8x_0\sim\mathcal N(0.8,0.16)$, noise var $0.36$: $\mathbb E[m\mid y]=y+0.36\,s(y)=0.2+0.36\cdot1.1538=0.6154$. Then $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]=0.6154/0.8=0.7692$. Direct check (Gaussian posterior, Ch 11): $\mathbb E[x_0\mid x_t]=1+\frac{\mathrm{Cov}(x_0,x_t)}{\mathrm{Var}(x_t)}(x_t-0.8)=1+\frac{0.8\cdot0.25}{0.52}(-0.6)=1-\frac{0.12}{0.52}=1-0.2308=0.7692$ ✓. (d) Optimal $\hat\epsilon=\mathbb E[\epsilon\mid x_t]$: from $x_t=0.8x_0+0.6\epsilon$, $\hat\epsilon=(x_t-0.8\hat x_0)/0.6=(0.2-0.6154)/0.6=-0.6923$; then $\hat x_0=(0.2-0.6\cdot(-0.6923))/0.8=(0.2+0.4154)/0.8=0.7692$ ✓ — all three views coincide.
    3. HARD Pick the right family. For each requirement, choose the best-suited model class from this chapter and justify in two sentences: (a) exact log-likelihoods for anomaly detection on tabular data; (b) real-time (single-forward-pass) generation of faces; (c) highest-fidelity text-conditioned images with a tunable fidelity/diversity knob; (d) discrete tokens for a downstream autoregressive multimodal model.
      Solution
      (a) Normalizing flow (or autoregressive): exact, cheap $\log p(x)$ per point; flows also give fast batched evaluation with no sequential loop. (b) GAN (or a 1-step distilled/rectified flow): one generator pass per sample; diffusion's iterative sampling misses the latency budget, though distillation closes the gap. (c) Diffusion / flow matching with classifier-free guidance: state-of-the-art fidelity, and $w$ is exactly the fidelity↔diversity knob. (d) VQ-VAE (or VQGAN): its codebook turns images/audio into token grids that a transformer can model jointly with text.

    Task 1 — VAE on a 1D mixture Train a tiny VAE (1D data, 1D latent) on samples from $\tfrac12\mathcal N(-2,0.5^2)+\tfrac12\mathcal N(2,0.5^2)$ and show that decoded prior samples are bimodal.
    Reference solution
    import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
def data(n):
    m = torch.where(torch.rand(n,1) < 0.5, -2.0, 2.0)
    return m + 0.5*torch.randn(n,1)

enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1,64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64,2))  # -> mu, logvar
dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(1,64), nn.Tanh(), nn.Linear(64,1))  # -> mean(x|z)
opt = torch.optim.Adam(list(enc.parameters())+list(dec.parameters()), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(4000):
    x = data(256)
    mu, logvar = enc(x).chunk(2, dim=1)
    z = mu + (0.5*logvar).exp()*torch.randn_like(mu)   # reparameterize
    xhat = dec(z)
    rec = 0.5*((x-xhat)**2).sum(1).mean()              # Gaussian NLL, sigma=1 (+const)
    kl  = 0.5*(logvar.exp() + mu**2 - 1 - logvar).sum(1).mean()
    loss = rec + kl
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print('loss', loss.item())
with torch.no_grad():
    samples = dec(torch.randn(4000,1)).squeeze()
print('fraction near -2:', ((samples+2).abs()<1).float().mean().item())
print('fraction near +2:', ((samples-2).abs()<1).float().mean().item())
# both fractions ~0.5: the prior decodes to both modes
    Task 2 — Denoising score matching + Langevin On the same 1D mixture, train $s_\theta(x,\sigma)$ with denoising score matching at two noise levels, then sample with annealed Langevin dynamics and check the two modes are recovered with roughly equal mass.
    Reference solution
    import torch, torch.nn as nn
torch.manual_seed(0)
def data(n):
    m = torch.where(torch.rand(n,1) < 0.5, -2.0, 2.0)
    return m + 0.5*torch.randn(n,1)

net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(2,128), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(128,128), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(128,1))
sigmas = [1.0, 0.3]
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=1e-3)
for step in range(6000):
    x = data(512)
    s = torch.tensor(sigmas)[torch.randint(0,2,(512,))].unsqueeze(1)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x)
    xt = x + s*eps
    target = -eps/s                       # = grad log q(xt|x)
    pred = net(torch.cat([xt, s], 1))
    loss = ((s**2)*(pred-target)**2).mean()   # sigma^2 weighting balances levels
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()

# annealed Langevin
x = 3*torch.randn(4000,1)
for sig in sigmas:
    eta = 0.05*sig**2
    for _ in range(200):
        with torch.no_grad():
            g = net(torch.cat([x, torch.full_like(x, sig)], 1))
        x = x + 0.5*eta*g + torch.sqrt(torch.tensor(eta))*torch.randn_like(x)
print('mass near -2:', ((x+2).abs()<1).float().mean().item())
print('mass near +2:', ((x-2).abs()<1).float().mean().item())  # both ~0.5
    Task 3 — A complete DDPM in 2D Implement DDPM (T=200, linear betas) on the two-moons dataset shape (or two Gaussians), with an $\epsilon$-prediction MLP conditioned on $t$; sample and verify visually/statistically.
    Reference solution
    import torch, torch.nn as nn, math
torch.manual_seed(0)
def data(n):                      # two Gaussians in 2D
    m = torch.where(torch.rand(n,1) < 0.5, -2.0, 2.0)
    return torch.cat([m, -m], 1)/1.5 + 0.3*torch.randn(n,2)

T = 200
beta = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.02, T)
alpha = 1 - beta
abar = torch.cumprod(alpha, 0)

net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(3,128), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(128,128), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(128,2))
opt = torch.optim.Adam(net.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
for step in range(8000):
    x0 = data(512)
    t  = torch.randint(0, T, (512,))
    a  = abar[t].unsqueeze(1)
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt  = a.sqrt()*x0 + (1-a).sqrt()*eps
    pred = net(torch.cat([xt, (t.float()/T).unsqueeze(1)], 1))
    loss = ((pred-eps)**2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print('train loss', loss.item())

# sampling
x = torch.randn(4000,2)
for t in reversed(range(T)):
    with torch.no_grad():
        e = net(torch.cat([x, torch.full((len(x),1), t/T)], 1))
    mean = (x - beta[t]/ (1-abar[t]).sqrt() * e) / alpha[t].sqrt()
    if t > 0:
        var = beta[t]*(1-abar[t-1])/(1-abar[t])
        x = mean + var.sqrt()*torch.randn_like(x)
    else:
        x = mean
print('sample mean', x.mean(0))            # ~ (0,0) by symmetry
print('mass in each mode:',
      ((x[:,0]<0).float().mean().item()), ((x[:,0]>0).float().mean().item()))
