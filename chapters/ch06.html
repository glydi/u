    Degree measure

    Degrees chop one full turn into 360 equal slices — a Babylonian convention that survived because 360 divides so nicely (by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12…). An angle in standard position starts on the positive $x$-axis and opens counterclockwise. Nothing in mathematics prefers 360 — it is a unit choice, like meters versus feet. The real object is a point on a circle: an angle names a direction, and any label that pins down the direction up to whole turns will do. Keep that picture; it is what makes "coterminal" obvious rather than a rule to memorize.

    Definition Full turn $= 360^\circ$; straight angle $=180^\circ$; right angle $=90^\circ$. Negative angles turn clockwise. Angles differing by a multiple of $360^\circ$ are coterminal — same terminal ray. One degree splits into 60 minutes ($'$), one minute into 60 seconds ($''$).
    Worked example Find the angle in $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$ coterminal with $-750^\circ$. Add turns: $-750 + 360 = -390$; $-390 + 360 = -30$; $-30 + 360 = 330^\circ$. So $-750^\circ$ points the same way as $330^\circ$.
    Pitfall Coterminal is not equal. $-30^\circ$ and $330^\circ$ point the same way, but as numbers they differ by a full turn. Anything that depends only on the terminal ray (sine, cosine, the direction a robot faces) treats them identically; anything that counts rotation (a motor that has spun five times, an unwound cable) does not. Decide which kind of quantity you have before you reduce mod $360^\circ$.
    Drill
    1. EASY Which angle in $[0^\circ,360^\circ)$ is coterminal with $400^\circ$?
      Solution
      $400 - 360 = 40^\circ$.
    2. MED Convert $12.3^\circ$ to degrees-minutes-seconds.
      Solution
      $0.3^\circ = 0.3 \times 60' = 18'$, so $12^\circ 18' 0''$.
    3. MED Convert $48^\circ 36' 45''$ to decimal degrees.
      Solution
      $36' = \tfrac{36}{60} = 0.6^\circ$ and $45'' = \tfrac{45}{3600} = 0.0125^\circ$, so $48.6125^\circ$.
    4. HARD The hour and minute hands at 3:20 — what is the angle between them?
      Solution
      Minute hand: $20/60 \cdot 360 = 120^\circ$ from 12. Hour hand: $3$ h $20$ min $= \tfrac{10}{3}$ h; $\tfrac{10}{3}\cdot 30^\circ = 100^\circ$ from 12. Angle $= 120 - 100 = 20^\circ$.
    5. HARD Through how many degrees does the Earth rotate while the minute hand of a clock sweeps one full turn? Which turns faster?
      Solution
      The minute hand takes 1 hour per turn; the Earth takes 24 hours per turn, so in that hour the Earth rotates $\tfrac{360^\circ}{24} = 15^\circ$. The minute hand is 24 times faster — and the hour hand ($360^\circ$ per 12 h $= 30^\circ$/h) is still twice as fast as the Earth.

    Radian measure

    A radian measures an angle by the arc it cuts on a unit circle: walk a distance of one radius along the rim and you've turned one radian. It is the natural unit — with radians, arc length is just $r\theta$ and calculus formulas lose all their ugly conversion factors. The deep reason: a radian is a ratio of two lengths (arc over radius), so it is dimensionless — a pure number. That is why you can feed it to a power series like $\theta - \tfrac{\theta^3}{6} + \cdots$ without units getting tangled, and why every library function, every derivative rule, and every formula from here to the end of the book silently assumes it.

    Definition $\theta$ (in radians) $= \dfrac{\text{arc length}}{\text{radius}}$. Full circle: $2\pi$ rad $= 360^\circ$, so $$1 \text{ rad} = \frac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx 57.30^\circ, \qquad x^\circ = x\cdot\frac{\pi}{180}\ \text{rad}.$$ Key values: $30^\circ = \tfrac\pi6$, $45^\circ = \tfrac\pi4$, $60^\circ = \tfrac\pi3$, $90^\circ = \tfrac\pi2$, $180^\circ = \pi$.
    Worked example Convert $135^\circ$: $135\cdot\frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$ rad. Convert $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ rad: $\frac{5\pi}{6}\cdot\frac{180}{\pi} = 150^\circ$.
    Think first Without a calculator: is one radian bigger or smaller than $60^\circ$? By roughly how much?
    Reveal
    Smaller. $1$ rad $= \tfrac{180^\circ}{\pi} \approx \tfrac{180}{3.14159} \approx 57.3^\circ$ — a radian is a $60^\circ$ slice trimmed by about $2.7^\circ$. Picture it: the arc has the same length as the radius, and an equilateral triangle ($60^\circ$) would need a straight chord of that length, so the angle must be slightly less than $60^\circ$.
    Pitfall Every formula in calculus ($\frac{d}{dx}\sin x = \cos x$, $\sin x \approx x$) assumes radians. Feed degrees into np.sin and you get garbage: np.sin(90) is $\sin(90\text{ rad}) \approx 0.894$, not 1.
    Where this goes The small-angle approximation $\sin x \approx x$ (radians only!) powers linearization in Ch 7 and the harmonic oscillator in Ch 10.
    Drill
    1. EASY Convert $270^\circ$ to radians.
      Solution
      $270\cdot\frac{\pi}{180} = \frac{3\pi}{2}$ — three quarters of a full turn.
    2. MED Convert 2 radians to degrees (decimal).
      Solution
      $2\cdot\frac{180}{\pi} \approx 114.59^\circ$.
    3. MED Which is larger: $\sin(1)$ or $\sin(1^\circ)$?
      Solution
      $\sin(1 \text{ rad}) \approx 0.8415$ while $\sin(1^\circ) = \sin(0.01745\text{ rad}) \approx 0.01745$. The radian value is nearly 50 times larger — a vivid reminder that the argument's unit matters enormously.
    4. HARD A wheel of radius 0.3 m rolls at 5 m/s without slipping. What is its angular speed in rad/s and rev/min?
      Solution
      $\omega = v/r = 5/0.3 = \tfrac{50}{3} \approx 16.67$ rad/s. In rev/min: $\frac{50/3}{2\pi}\cdot 60 = \frac{1000}{2\pi} \approx 159.2$ rpm.

    Arc length

    In radians, the arc-length formula collapses to a product: distance along the rim equals radius times angle. This is why radians are the right unit — the circle's geometry becomes multiplication by one number. Double the angle, double the arc; double the radius, double the arc. Everything from odometry (how far did the wheel's rim travel?) to navigation (how long is a degree of latitude?) is this one product.

    Definition For central angle $\theta$ in radians on a circle of radius $r$: $$s = r\theta.$$ Sector area: $A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta$. (Compare the degree versions in Ch 5 — the $\frac{\theta}{360}\cdot 2\pi$ fraction is exactly the radian measure.)
    Worked example A pendulum of length 2 m swings through $0.3$ rad. Arc traveled by the bob: $s = 2 \times 0.3 = 0.6$ m. Sector swept: $A = \tfrac12\cdot 4 \cdot 0.3 = 0.6$ m².
    Where this goes $s = r\theta$ is the baby case of arc length for general curves, which Ch 7 computes with the integral $\int\sqrt{1 + (y')^2}\,dx$ — for a circle that integral collapses back to $r\theta$. The sector area $\tfrac12 r^2\theta$ reappears as the polar-coordinates area element in Ch 9.
    Drill
    1. EASY Arc for $r = 5$, $\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{5}$.
      Solution
      $s = r\theta = 5\cdot\tfrac\pi5 = \pi \approx 3.14$.
    2. MED An arc of length 7 subtends an angle of 1.4 rad. Find the radius and the sector area.
      Solution
      $r = s/\theta = 7/1.4 = 5$. $A = \tfrac12\cdot 25\cdot 1.4 = 17.5$.
    3. MED A nautical mile is one minute of arc of latitude on the Earth ($R \approx 6371$ km). How long is it?
      Solution
      $\theta = \tfrac{1}{60}\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{180} = \tfrac{\pi}{10800}$ rad. $s = 6371 \cdot \tfrac{\pi}{10800} \approx 1.853$ km — the official value is 1.852 km.
    4. HARD Two gears mesh: radii 6 cm and 4 cm. The large gear turns $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ rad. Through what angle does the small one turn?
      Solution
      Meshed gears share rim distance: $s = 6\cdot\tfrac\pi3 = 2\pi$ cm. Small gear: $\theta = s/r = 2\pi/4 = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$ rad (90°), turning the opposite way.

    Unit circle

    The unit circle — radius 1, centered at the origin — is the master diagram of trigonometry. Walk counterclockwise from $(1,0)$ by angle $\theta$; wherever you stand, your coordinates are $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Every identity in this chapter is a statement about this picture. Read the symmetries off the circle directly: reflect across the $x$-axis and $\theta \mapsto -\theta$ flips the height but not the width (sine odd, cosine even); reflect across the $y$-axis and $\theta \mapsto \pi - \theta$ keeps the height (that is why $\sin\theta = \sin(\pi-\theta)$); a quarter-turn swaps the two coordinates (the cofunction identities). Fluency here means never memorizing a sign rule again — you just look.

    Definition The unit circle is $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. For any real $\theta$, the terminal point of angle $\theta$ (standard position) is $P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Immediate consequences: $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$; both functions are periodic with period $2\pi$; $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$ (even), $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$ (odd). The reference angle of θ is the acute angle its ray makes with the $x$-axis: it fixes the magnitudes of $\cos$ and $\sin$, and the quadrant fixes the signs. This two-step recipe — magnitude from the reference angle, sign from the quadrant — evaluates any special angle in seconds.
    Worked example The key first-quadrant points: $\theta = \tfrac\pi6$: $(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}, \tfrac12)$; $\theta = \tfrac\pi4$: $(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}, \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2})$; $\theta = \tfrac\pi3$: $(\tfrac12, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2})$. For $\theta = \tfrac{2\pi}{3}$ (quadrant II): reference angle $\tfrac\pi3$, cosine negative there, so $\big({-\tfrac12}, \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\big)$.

    1. EASY Terminal point of $\theta = \pi$?
      Solution
      $(-1, 0)$: $\cos\pi = -1$, $\sin\pi = 0$ — half-way around the circle from $(1,0)$.
    2. MED Exact coordinates for $\theta = \tfrac{7\pi}{6}$.
      Solution
      Reference angle $\tfrac\pi6$; quadrant III (both negative): $\big({-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}}, {-\tfrac12}\big)$.
    3. HARD $\sin\theta = \tfrac35$ and $\theta$ is in quadrant II. Find $\cos\theta$ exactly.

    Sine

    Sine is height on the unit circle: how far above the horizontal axis you are after turning through θ. In a right triangle, that same ratio is opposite over hypotenuse. As θ runs, sine traces the wave that describes sound, light, springs, and alternating current. Why does one shape rule all of physics? Because anything that oscillates near a stable equilibrium feels a restoring pull proportional to its displacement, and the function whose acceleration is proportional to minus itself is exactly the sine wave (Ch 7 makes this precise). Sine is not one wave among many — it is the wave that all small oscillations become.

    Definition $\sin\theta = y$-coordinate of the terminal point on the unit circle. Right-triangle version (acute $\theta$): $\sin\theta = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$. Range $[-1, 1]$; period $2\pi$; odd function; zeros at integer multiples of $\pi$; maximum 1 at $\theta = \tfrac\pi2 + 2k\pi$.
    Worked example A ramp rises at $\theta$ with $\sin\theta = \tfrac{3}{10}$ and length 10 m: vertical rise $= 10\sin\theta = 3$ m. And exact values to memorize: $\sin 0 = 0$, $\sin\tfrac\pi6 = \tfrac12$, $\sin\tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$, $\sin\tfrac\pi3 = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\sin\tfrac\pi2 = 1$ — the pattern $\tfrac{\sqrt{0}}2, \tfrac{\sqrt1}2, \tfrac{\sqrt2}2, \tfrac{\sqrt3}2, \tfrac{\sqrt4}2$ — five values that, with symmetry, cover every special angle on the circle.
    Where this goes Sinusoids are the atoms of Fourier analysis (Ch 14): every signal decomposes into sines and cosines. The same chapter builds sinusoidal positional encodings — a transformer tells token 17 from token 18 by a bank of sines at geometrically spaced frequencies — and RoPE, which encodes position by rotating embedding pairs through position-proportional angles.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\sin\tfrac{3\pi}{2} = ?$
      Solution
      Bottom of the circle, $(0,-1)$: the $y$-coordinate is $-1$.
    2. MED Solve $\sin\theta = \tfrac12$ for $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      $\theta = \tfrac\pi6$ and $\theta = \pi - \tfrac\pi6 = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}$.
    3. MED Order $\sin 1$, $\sin 2$, $\sin 3$ (radians) from smallest to largest, without a calculator.
      Solution
      All three angles lie in $(0, \pi)$, where sine is positive, rising until $\tfrac\pi2 \approx 1.571$ and falling after. $\sin 3$ is nearly at the zero at $\pi \approx 3.14$, so it is small ($\approx 0.141$). Between $\sin 1$ and $\sin 2$: use symmetry, $\sin 2 = \sin(\pi - 2) = \sin(1.14)$, and $1.14 > 1$ on the rising part, so $\sin 2 > \sin 1$. Order: $\sin 3 < \sin 1 < \sin 2$ ($0.141 < 0.841 < 0.909$).
    4. HARD A Ferris wheel of radius 20 m, center 22 m up, turns once per 60 s starting at the bottom. Write the rider's height $h(t)$ and find $h(10)$.
      Solution
      Height: $h(t) = 22 - 20\cos\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi t}{60}\big)$ (cosine starts at the bottom). $h(10) = 22 - 20\cos\tfrac{\pi}{3} = 22 - 10 = 12$ m.

    Cosine

    Cosine is horizontal position on the unit circle — sine's twin, a quarter turn ahead. In a right triangle it is adjacent over hypotenuse. Cosine measures alignment, which is why the dot product formula of Ch 5 runs on it. Think of it as a "same-direction score": $+1$ for parallel, $0$ for perpendicular, $-1$ for opposite — a dimmer switch for how much of one direction lives inside another. Whenever a model asks "how similar are these two things?", there is usually a cosine underneath.

    Definition $\cos\theta = x$-coordinate of the terminal point. Right-triangle version: $\cos\theta = \dfrac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}$. Range $[-1,1]$; period $2\pi$; even function; zeros at $\tfrac\pi2 + k\pi$. Cofunction identity: $\cos\theta = \sin(\tfrac\pi2 - \theta)$, and $\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \tfrac\pi2)$.
    Worked example A 6 m ladder makes $\theta = 65^\circ$ with the ground. Foot-to-wall distance $= 6\cos 65^\circ \approx 6 \times 0.4226 = 2.54$ m; height reached $= 6\sin 65^\circ \approx 5.44$ m. Check: $2.54^2 + 5.44^2 \approx 6.45 + 29.6 \approx 36$. ✓
    Pitfall "Adjacent over hypotenuse" only makes sense for acute angles — there is no right triangle with a $150^\circ$ corner. The unit-circle definition ($x$-coordinate) is the real one; the triangle ratio is its special case. When an angle leaves $(0^\circ, 90^\circ)$, reason from the circle, not the triangle.
    Where this goes Cosine similarity $\frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\|\mathbf{u}\|\|\mathbf{v}\|}$ ranks embeddings in retrieval systems (Ch 31); positional encodings (Ch 14) are interleaved sines and cosines.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\cos\tfrac{2\pi}{3} = ?$
      Solution
      Reference $\tfrac\pi3$, quadrant II: $-\tfrac12$.
    2. EASY $\cos(-\tfrac\pi3) = ?$
      Solution
      Cosine is even: $\cos(-\tfrac\pi3) = \cos\tfrac\pi3 = \tfrac12$.
    3. MED Solve $\cos\theta = -\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      Cosine is negative in quadrants II and III; reference angle $\tfrac\pi4$: $\theta = \pi - \tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}$ and $\pi + \tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac{5\pi}{4}$.
    4. HARD Two unit vectors have dot product $-0.5$. What is the angle between them, and what is $\|\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}\|$?
      Solution
      $\cos\theta = -0.5 \Rightarrow \theta = 120^\circ$. $\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\|^2 = 1 + 1 - 2(-0.5) = 3$, so $\|\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{v}\| = \sqrt3$.

    Tangent

    Tangent is slope: the ratio of rise (sine) to run (cosine) of the ray at angle θ. A line making angle θ with the $x$-axis has slope exactly $\tan\theta$. It blows up where cosine dies — at vertical. That blow-up is honest: as a ramp tilts toward vertical, its rise-per-unit-run really does grow without bound, and no finite slope describes a vertical line. Tangent is the bridge between angles (bounded, periodic) and slopes (unbounded) — and its inverse, arctan, is the bridge back.

    Definition $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}$, defined for $\cos\theta \ne 0$ (undefined at $\theta = \tfrac\pi2 + k\pi$, where the graph has vertical asymptotes). Period $\pi$ (not $2\pi$); odd; range all of $\mathbb{R}$. Values: $\tan\tfrac\pi6 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$, $\tan\tfrac\pi4 = 1$, $\tan\tfrac\pi3 = \sqrt3$. Near zero, $\tan\theta \approx \theta$ (radians) — like sine, but approaching from above rather than below.
    Worked example From 50 m away, the top of a tower sits at an elevation angle of $32^\circ$. Height $= 50\tan 32^\circ \approx 50 \times 0.6249 = 31.2$ m (plus eye height). This is the surveyor's whole trade: an angle you can measure plus one distance you can pace off gives the length you cannot reach.
    Pitfall $\tan$ has period $\pi$: $\tan(\theta + \pi) = \tan\theta$. Solving $\tan\theta = 1$ on $[0, 2\pi)$ gives two answers, $\tfrac\pi4$ and $\tfrac{5\pi}{4}$ — not just one.
    Where this goes The derivative of Ch 7 is a limit of slopes, i.e. of tangents of tilt angles — the name "tangent line" is this function's geometry. And $\tan$'s inverse arctan, with its S-shaped graph squashing $\mathbb{R}$ into a bounded interval, is a close cousin of the sigmoid squashing functions of Ch 21.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\tan\tfrac{3\pi}{4} = ?$
      Solution
      $\frac{\sin(3\pi/4)}{\cos(3\pi/4)} = \frac{\sqrt2/2}{-\sqrt2/2} = -1$.
    2. MED A road climbs 8 m per 100 m horizontal. What angle does it make with the horizontal?
      Solution
      $\tan\theta = 0.08 \Rightarrow \theta = \arctan 0.08 \approx 4.57^\circ$.
    3. MED For which $\theta \in [0, 2\pi)$ is $\tan\theta$ undefined, and what does the graph do there?
      Solution
      Where $\cos\theta = 0$: $\theta = \tfrac\pi2$ and $\tfrac{3\pi}2$. The graph has vertical asymptotes: $\tan\theta \to +\infty$ approaching from the left, $-\infty$ from the right.
    4. HARD If $\tan\theta = \tfrac{5}{12}$ with $\theta$ in quadrant III, find $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$.
      Solution
      Reference triangle 5-12-13. Quadrant III: both negative. $\sin\theta = -\tfrac{5}{13}$, $\cos\theta = -\tfrac{12}{13}$. Check: ratio $= \tfrac{5}{12}$ ✓, squares sum to 1 ✓.

    Cosecant

    Cosecant is the reciprocal of sine — big when sine is small. Where sine crosses zero, cosecant shoots off to infinity, producing a graph of repeating U-shapes riding the sine wave's peaks and valleys. You will rarely meet cosecant in machine learning, but it earns its keep in calculus (certain integrals) and in any formula where "one over a projection" appears — the length of a ladder that must reach a given height at angle θ is $h\csc\theta$.

    Definition $\csc\theta = \dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}$ (undefined at $\theta = k\pi$). Range: $(-\infty, -1] \cup [1, \infty)$; period $2\pi$; odd. Right triangle: hypotenuse/opposite.
    Worked example $\csc\tfrac\pi6 = \dfrac{1}{\sin(\pi/6)} = \dfrac{1}{1/2} = 2$ — a ladder leaning at only $30^\circ$ must be twice as long as the height it reaches. And $\csc\tfrac\pi2 = 1$ — the smallest positive value cosecant ever takes, at sine's peak.
    Pitfall $\csc x$ is $\dfrac{1}{\sin x}$, but the calculator button $\sin^{-1}$ is not it — that button is arcsin, the inverse function. The notation $\sin^{-1}x$ collides with the reciprocal reading; this book writes $\arcsin$ for the inverse and $\csc$ for the reciprocal and never uses $\sin^{-1}$.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\csc\tfrac{\pi}{4} = ?$
      Solution
      $\frac{1}{\sqrt2/2} = \sqrt2$.
    2. MED Why is there no $\theta$ with $\csc\theta = \tfrac12$?
      Solution
      That would need $\sin\theta = 2$, impossible since $|\sin\theta| \le 1$. Cosecant never enters $(-1, 1)$.
    3. MED On the interval $(0, \pi)$, where does $\csc\theta$ attain its minimum, and what is it?
      Solution
      On $(0,\pi)$, $\sin\theta$ is positive and peaks at $\theta = \tfrac\pi2$ with value 1, so its reciprocal bottoms out there: minimum $\csc\tfrac\pi2 = 1$. This is the "shortest ladder" fact: to reach a fixed height, the ladder $h\csc\theta$ is shortest when vertical.
    4. HARD If $\csc\theta = -\tfrac{13}{5}$ and $\cos\theta > 0$, find $\tan\theta$.
      Solution
      $\sin\theta = -\tfrac{5}{13}$; sine negative, cosine positive → quadrant IV, $\cos\theta = \tfrac{12}{13}$. $\tan\theta = -\tfrac{5}{12}$.

    Secant

    Secant is the reciprocal of cosine. Its name comes from the Latin for "cutting" — geometrically it is the length of the line from the origin, along angle θ, out to where it cuts the vertical tangent line at $x=1$. So $\sec\theta$ answers "how much longer than the radius is the slanted reach?" — at $\theta = 0$ no longer at all ($\sec 0 = 1$), and as θ tilts toward $90^\circ$ the reach grows without bound.

    Definition $\sec\theta = \dfrac{1}{\cos\theta}$ (undefined at $\theta = \tfrac\pi2 + k\pi$). Range $(-\infty,-1]\cup[1,\infty)$; period $2\pi$; even. Identity preview: $1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta$.
    Worked example $\sec\tfrac\pi3 = \dfrac{1}{1/2} = 2$; $\sec\pi = \dfrac{1}{-1} = -1$; $\sec 0 = 1$.
    Think first True or false: $\sec\theta \ge 1$ for every θ where it is defined.
    Reveal
    False. Only $|\sec\theta| \ge 1$ is true. Wherever cosine is negative (quadrants II and III), secant is $\le -1$ — e.g. $\sec\pi = -1$, $\sec\tfrac{2\pi}3 = -2$.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\sec\tfrac{\pi}{4} = ?$
      Solution
      $\sqrt2$.
    2. MED Solve $\sec\theta = 2$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      $\cos\theta = \tfrac12$: $\theta = \tfrac\pi3$ and $\tfrac{5\pi}3$.
    3. MED Verify $1 + \tan^2\tfrac\pi3 = \sec^2\tfrac\pi3$ numerically.
      Solution
      $1 + (\sqrt3)^2 = 4$ and $\sec^2\tfrac\pi3 = 2^2 = 4$. ✓
    4. HARD A wall of height 1 stands at $x = 1$. A ray from the origin at angle $\theta$ hits the wall's line $x=1$ at height $\tan\theta$, at distance $\sec\theta$ from the origin. Prove it.
      Solution
      The ray is $(t\cos\theta, t\sin\theta)$. It reaches $x = 1$ when $t\cos\theta = 1$, i.e. $t = \sec\theta$ — and $t$ is exactly the distance from the origin. Height there: $t\sin\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \tan\theta$. $\blacksquare$ (This picture is where the names tangent and secant come from.)

    Cotangent

    Cotangent is tangent flipped: run over rise, cosine over sine. It measures the slope's reciprocal, blows up where sine vanishes, and completes the six-pack of trig functions. Its graph mirrors tangent's: the same repeating branches between vertical asymptotes, but decreasing on each branch, with asymptotes where tangent has zeros and zeros where tangent has asymptotes.

    Definition $\cot\theta = \dfrac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} = \dfrac{1}{\tan\theta}$ (undefined at $\theta = k\pi$). Period $\pi$; odd; range $\mathbb{R}$. Identity: $1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta$. Cofunction: $\cot\theta = \tan(\tfrac\pi2 - \theta)$.
    Worked example $\cot\tfrac\pi6 = \frac{\cos(\pi/6)}{\sin(\pi/6)} = \frac{\sqrt3/2}{1/2} = \sqrt3$ — the reciprocal of $\tan\tfrac\pi6 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$, and equal to $\tan\tfrac\pi3$, its cofunction partner.
    Pitfall $\cot\theta = \tfrac1{\tan\theta}$ fails where $\tan\theta = 0$ or is undefined. At $\theta = \tfrac\pi2$: $\tan$ is undefined but $\cot\tfrac\pi2 = 0$ is perfectly fine. Use $\cos/\sin$ as the real definition.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\cot\tfrac{\pi}{4} = ?$
      Solution
      $1$.
    2. EASY $\cot\tfrac{\pi}{2} = ?$
      Solution
      $\frac{\cos(\pi/2)}{\sin(\pi/2)} = \frac{0}{1} = 0$ — defined, even though $\tan\tfrac\pi2$ is not.
    3. MED Solve $\cot\theta = -\sqrt3$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      $\tan\theta = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$: reference $\tfrac\pi6$, quadrants II and IV: $\theta = \tfrac{5\pi}{6}, \tfrac{11\pi}{6}$.
    4. HARD Show $\cot\theta + \tan\theta = \dfrac{2}{\sin 2\theta}$ (you may use $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$).
      Solution
      $\cot\theta + \tan\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta} + \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = \frac{1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = \frac{2}{2\sin\theta\cos\theta} = \frac{2}{\sin 2\theta}$. $\blacksquare$

    Trigonometric graphs

    Unroll the unit circle along the horizontal axis and sine's height traces a wave; cosine is the same wave shifted left by a quarter period. Tangent draws repeating S-curves between vertical asymptotes. Reading these graphs — where they peak, cross zero, repeat — is reading the behavior of any oscillating system. Train the reflexes: a solution count is an intersection count between a wave and a horizontal line; a sign question is "which side of the axis is the wave on here?"; a symmetry claim is a fold of the paper. Most trig "computations" dissolve into a five-second glance at the right graph.

    Definition $y = \sin x$: wave through the origin, peaks at $\tfrac\pi2 + 2k\pi$, period $2\pi$. $y = \cos x$: starts at its peak $(0,1)$; $\cos x = \sin(x + \tfrac\pi2)$. $y = \tan x$: period $\pi$, zeros at $k\pi$, vertical asymptotes at $\tfrac\pi2 + k\pi$, increasing on each branch. Sine and cosine are bounded, continuous, and infinitely smooth everywhere; tangent is smooth on each branch but unbounded — the qualitative split that decides which is safe inside a numerical model.
    Worked example Sketch $y = \sin x$ on $[0, 2\pi]$ from five anchor points: $(0,0)$, $(\tfrac\pi2, 1)$, $(\pi, 0)$, $(\tfrac{3\pi}{2}, -1)$, $(2\pi, 0)$ — then connect with the smooth S-curves. Cosine uses the same five heights shifted: starts at 1, hits 0 at $\tfrac\pi2$, $-1$ at $\pi$.

    Amplitude

    Amplitude is how tall the wave is: the distance from the midline to a peak. Turn up a speaker's volume and you scale the sound wave's amplitude; the pitch (period) doesn't change. Amplitude, period, phase, midline — the four knobs $A, B, C, D$ of $A\sin(Bx+C)+D$ — are independent: each moves one visual feature of the wave and leaves the others alone. This section and the next two take them one at a time.

    Definition For $y = A\sin(Bx + C) + D$ or the cosine version, the amplitude is $|A|$: the graph oscillates between $D - |A|$ and $D + |A|$ around midline $y = D$. Negative $A$ flips the wave upside down. Physically, amplitude carries the energy: a wave's power scales with the amplitude squared, so doubling amplitude quadruples power.
    Worked example $y = -3\sin x + 1$: amplitude $|-3| = 3$, midline $y = 1$, range $[-2, 4]$, and the flip means it starts heading down from $(0, 1)$.
    Pitfall Amplitude is the distance from the midline to a peak — half the peak-to-trough distance — not the maximum value. A wave living between $-7$ and $3$ has amplitude 5, not 3, because its midline sits at $-2$. Find the midline first, always.
    Drill
    1. EASY Amplitude and range of $y = 5\cos x$?
      Solution
      Amplitude 5, range $[-5, 5]$.
    2. MED A wave oscillates between $-7$ and $3$. Find its amplitude and midline.
      Solution
      Midline $D = \frac{-7+3}{2} = -2$; amplitude $= 3 - (-2) = 5$.
    3. MED A tide oscillates sinusoidally between 0.8 m and 3.2 m. Write one possible height function with period 12 h, high tide at $t = 0$.
      Solution
      Midline $\frac{0.8+3.2}{2} = 2$ m, amplitude $1.2$ m, $B = \frac{2\pi}{12} = \frac\pi6$. High tide at $t=0$ wants a cosine: $h(t) = 2 + 1.2\cos\frac{\pi t}{6}$. Check: $h(0) = 3.2$ ✓, $h(6) = 2 - 1.2 = 0.8$ ✓.
    4. HARD Find the maximum value of $f(x) = 3\sin x + 4\cos x$ and where it occurs.
      Solution
      Write as $R\sin(x+\varphi)$ with $R = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = 5$, $\cos\varphi = \tfrac35, \sin\varphi = \tfrac45$. Max value 5, at $x + \varphi = \tfrac\pi2$, i.e. $x = \tfrac\pi2 - \arctan\tfrac43 \approx 0.6435$ rad. (Check: $3\sin(0.6435)+4\cos(0.6435) = 3(0.6)+4(0.8) = 5$. ✓)
    What is the maximum value of $y = -2\sin x + 3$?
    Amplitude $|-2| = 2$ around midline 3: the wave lives in $[1, 5]$, peaking at 5 (where $\sin x = -1$).

    Period

    The period is how long the wave takes to repeat. The factor $B$ inside $\sin(Bx)$ compresses time: bigger $B$, faster oscillation, shorter period. Frequency is the reciprocal — cycles per unit. Get the three vocabularies straight once: period $T$ (seconds per cycle), frequency $f = 1/T$ (cycles per second, hertz), angular frequency $\omega = 2\pi f$ (radians per second). They carry the same information; physics prefers $\omega$ because formulas stay clean in radians.

    Definition $y = A\sin(Bx + C) + D$ has period $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{|B|}$ (for tangent: $\pi/|B|$). Frequency $f = 1/T$; angular frequency $\omega = B = 2\pi f$.
    Worked example Mains electricity: $V(t) = 325\sin(100\pi t)$ volts. Period $T = \frac{2\pi}{100\pi} = 0.02$ s, so frequency $= 50$ Hz. Amplitude 325 V (the "230 V" rating is the RMS value $325/\sqrt2 \approx 230$ — root-mean-square, computed from the average of $\sin^2$ being $\tfrac12$, a double-angle fact from two sections ahead).
    Pitfall Period $2\pi/B$, not $2\pi B$. Check with $B=2$: $\sin 2x$ wiggles twice as fast, so its period must be smaller, $\pi$.
    Where this goes Frequency is the axis of the Fourier transform (Ch 14): a spectrum is a plot of "how much of each frequency" a signal contains. Sinusoidal positional encodings (Ch 14) use a geometric ladder of periods — from a few tokens to tens of thousands — so that every pair of positions is distinguished by some frequency in the bank.
    Drill
    1. EASY Period of $y = \cos 4x$?
      Solution
      $\frac{2\pi}{4} = \frac\pi2$.
    2. EASY Frequency (in Hz) of $y = \sin(6\pi t)$, $t$ in seconds?
      Solution
      $T = \frac{2\pi}{6\pi} = \frac13$ s, so $f = 3$ Hz.
    3. MED Write a sine with amplitude 2 and period 6.
      Solution
      $B = \frac{2\pi}{6} = \frac\pi3$: $y = 2\sin\frac{\pi x}{3}$.
    4. HARD What is the period of $f(x) = \sin 2x + \cos 3x$?
      Solution
      Periods $\pi$ and $\frac{2\pi}{3}$; the sum repeats at the least common multiple: $\text{lcm}(\pi, \tfrac{2\pi}3) = 2\pi$ (check: $\pi\cdot2 = 2\pi = \tfrac{2\pi}3\cdot 3$). Period $2\pi$. (When the two periods have an irrational ratio — say $\sin x + \sin \pi x$ — no common multiple exists and the sum never repeats at all: it is "quasiperiodic".)

    Phase shift

    Phase shift slides the wave left or right without changing its shape. Two waves with the same period but different phases are the same song started at different moments — and their interference (add them!) depends entirely on the phase gap. In phase (gap 0), crests meet crests and the sum doubles; out of phase (gap $\pi$), crests meet troughs and the waves annihilate — that is how noise-cancelling headphones work. Phase is the invisible coordinate: two signals can look identical on a spectrum and behave completely differently because of it.

    Definition $y = A\sin(B(x - \varphi)) + D$ is the basic wave shifted right by $\varphi$. In the expanded form $A\sin(Bx + C)$, the shift is $\varphi = -C/B$ (factor out $B$ first!).
    Worked example $y = \sin(2x - \tfrac{\pi}{3}) = \sin\!\big(2(x - \tfrac\pi6)\big)$: period $\pi$, shifted right by $\tfrac\pi6$ — not by $\tfrac\pi3$. The first peak sits where $2x - \tfrac\pi3 = \tfrac\pi2$, i.e. $x = \tfrac{5\pi}{12}$.
    Where this goes Phase is exactly what RoPE (Ch 14) manipulates: each token's query/key pair is a tiny wave, and position $p$ shifts its phase by $p\theta_k$. Two tokens' dot product then depends only on their phase difference — i.e. their relative position — which is the whole trick.
    Explore

    Shape the wave y = A sin(Bx + C) + D and watch amplitude, period, phase and midline act independently.

    Drill
    1. EASY Phase shift of $y = \cos(x - \tfrac\pi4)$?
      Solution
      Right by $\tfrac\pi4$.
    2. MED By what shift does cosine become sine?
      Solution
      $\sin x = \cos(x - \tfrac\pi2)$: shift cosine right by $\tfrac\pi2$. (Check at $x = \tfrac\pi2$: $\cos 0 = 1 = \sin\tfrac\pi2$. ✓) Every sinusoid is every other sinusoid in disguise — amplitude, period, phase are the only degrees of freedom.
    3. MED Phase shift and period of $y = 4\sin(3x + \pi)$?
      Solution
      $y = 4\sin(3(x + \tfrac\pi3))$: period $\tfrac{2\pi}3$, shift left $\tfrac\pi3$.
    4. HARD Two speakers play $\sin(\omega t)$ and $\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$. For what phase gaps $\varphi$ is the combined sound loudest, and when does it vanish?
      Solution
      Sum-to-product: $\sin(\omega t) + \sin(\omega t + \varphi) = 2\cos\tfrac{\varphi}{2}\sin(\omega t + \tfrac{\varphi}{2})$ — a wave of amplitude $2|\cos\tfrac\varphi2|$. Loudest (amplitude 2) at $\varphi = 2k\pi$ (in phase); silence at $\varphi = \pi + 2k\pi$ (crests cancel troughs).
    5. HARD Daylight in some city: minimum 8 h on Dec 21 (day 355), maximum 16 h on Jun 21 (day 172). Model daylight $L(d)$ with a cosine.
      Solution
      Midline 12, amplitude 4, period 365. Peak at $d = 172$: $L(d) = 12 + 4\cos\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{365}(d - 172)\big)$. Check Dec 21: $d - 172 = 183 \approx$ half a period, cosine $\approx -1$, $L \approx 8$. ✓

    Trigonometric identities

    An identity is an equation true for every allowed angle — a statement about the functions themselves, not a puzzle to solve for θ. Identities let you rewrite trig expressions into whatever form the problem wants, the way algebra lets you factor and expand. Strategy beats memorization here: convert everything to sines and cosines, look for a Pythagorean substitution, and work from the messier side toward the cleaner one. A quick numeric spot-check (plug in $x = 0.7$, say) catches most wrong "identities" before you waste ten minutes proving something false — one random angle where the two sides disagree is a complete disproof, while agreement at a few random angles is strong (though not conclusive) evidence.

    Definition Core toolkit: reciprocal ($\csc = 1/\sin$, $\sec = 1/\cos$, $\cot = 1/\tan$), quotient ($\tan = \sin/\cos$), Pythagorean ($\sin^2 + \cos^2 = 1$ and friends), even–odd ($\cos(-x) = \cos x$, $\sin(-x) = -\sin x$), and cofunction ($\sin(\tfrac\pi2 - x) = \cos x$). To prove an identity, transform one side into the other using known identities — never assume the equation and manipulate both sides.
    Worked example Prove $\dfrac{\sin x}{1 + \cos x} = \dfrac{1 - \cos x}{\sin x}$. Start left, multiply by $\frac{1-\cos x}{1-\cos x}$: $$\frac{\sin x (1-\cos x)}{(1+\cos x)(1-\cos x)} = \frac{\sin x(1-\cos x)}{1 - \cos^2 x} = \frac{\sin x (1-\cos x)}{\sin^2 x} = \frac{1-\cos x}{\sin x}. \; \blacksquare$$
    Pitfall $\sin^2 x$ means $(\sin x)^2$, never $\sin(x^2)$ — and $\sin(a+b) \ne \sin a + \sin b$. Sine is not linear; the addition formulas below give the true expansion.
    Drill
    1. EASY Simplify $\sin x \cot x$.
      Solution
      $\sin x \cdot \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} = \cos x$.
    2. EASY Simplify $(1 - \cos x)(1 + \cos x)$.
      Solution
      Difference of squares: $1 - \cos^2 x = \sin^2 x$.
    3. MED Prove $\sec^2 x + \csc^2 x = \sec^2 x \csc^2 x$.
      Solution
      Left $= \frac{1}{\cos^2} + \frac{1}{\sin^2} = \frac{\sin^2 + \cos^2}{\sin^2\cos^2} = \frac{1}{\sin^2 x\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x\csc^2 x$. $\blacksquare$
    4. HARD Prove $\dfrac{1 + \sin x}{\cos x} = \dfrac{\cos x}{1 - \sin x}$ and state where both sides are defined.
      Solution
      Cross-multiplied claim: $(1+\sin x)(1-\sin x) = \cos^2 x$, i.e. $1 - \sin^2 x = \cos^2 x$ — Pythagorean, true. To keep the proof one-directional: $\frac{1+\sin x}{\cos x} = \frac{(1+\sin x)(1-\sin x)}{\cos x (1-\sin x)} = \frac{\cos^2 x}{\cos x(1-\sin x)} = \frac{\cos x}{1-\sin x}$. Defined when $\cos x \ne 0$ and $\sin x \ne 1$: all $x \ne \tfrac\pi2 + k\pi$.

    Pythagorean identities

    The equation of the unit circle, $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, read with $x = \cos\theta$, $y = \sin\theta$, becomes the most-used identity in mathematics. Dividing it by $\cos^2$ or $\sin^2$ spawns two siblings. Its power is a trade: it converts between $\sin^2$ and $\cos^2$ at will, so knowing one trig value of an angle (plus a quadrant) unlocks all six. It is also why "a point on the unit circle" and "a pair $(c, s)$ with $c^2 + s^2 = 1$" are the same thing — the algebraic passport of the circle.

    Definition $$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1, \qquad 1 + \tan^2\theta = \sec^2\theta, \qquad 1 + \cot^2\theta = \csc^2\theta.$$ Rearrangements you'll constantly use: $\sin^2\theta = 1 - \cos^2\theta$, $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta$.
    Worked example Given $\tan\theta = 2$ with $\theta$ in quadrant III, find all six values. $\sec^2\theta = 1 + 4 = 5 \Rightarrow \sec\theta = -\sqrt5$ (cosine negative in QIII), $\cos\theta = -\tfrac{1}{\sqrt5}$. $\sin\theta = \tan\theta\cos\theta = -\tfrac{2}{\sqrt5}$. Then $\csc\theta = -\tfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$, $\cot\theta = \tfrac12$. Check: $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = \tfrac45 + \tfrac15 = 1$. ✓ The workflow generalizes: one value plus one quadrant determines everything, and the Pythagorean identities are the conversion machinery.
    Think first What is $\sin^4\theta - \cos^4\theta$ in terms of $\cos 2\theta$-free simple functions? Try factoring first.
    Reveal
    $(\sin^2 - \cos^2)(\sin^2 + \cos^2) = \sin^2\theta - \cos^2\theta$ (which also equals $-\cos 2\theta$).
    Drill
    1. EASY If $\cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{3}$, find $\sin^2\theta$.
      Solution
      $1 - \tfrac19 = \tfrac89$.
    2. MED Simplify $\dfrac{1 - \cos^2 x}{\tan x \, \cos x}$ (assume all defined).
      Solution
      Numerator $=\sin^2 x$; denominator $= \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\cos x = \sin x$. Result: $\sin x$.
    3. MED If $\tan\theta + \cot\theta = 3$, find $\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
      Solution
      $\tan\theta + \cot\theta = \frac{\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = \frac{1}{\sin\theta\cos\theta} = 3$, so $\sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac13$.
    4. HARD If $\sin\theta + \cos\theta = \tfrac{7}{5}$, find $\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\sin^3\theta + \cos^3\theta$.
      Solution
      Square: $1 + 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac{49}{25} \Rightarrow \sin\theta\cos\theta = \tfrac{12}{25}$. Sum of cubes: $(\sin+\cos)(\sin^2 - \sin\cos + \cos^2) = \tfrac75\big(1 - \tfrac{12}{25}\big) = \tfrac75\cdot\tfrac{13}{25} = \tfrac{91}{125}$.

    Angle addition formulas

    What is the sine of a sum of two angles? Not the sum of the sines — rotation composes in a twisted way, and these formulas are the exact bookkeeping. They are secretly matrix multiplication of two rotations (Ch 8), and every later identity falls out of them. Here is the picture: rotate the plane by $b$, then by $a$. The point that started at angle 0 ends at angle $a+b$, but its coordinates got there by two successive coordinate mixings — and "mixing coordinates twice" is precisely what the right-hand sides compute. Once you own these two formulas, double-angle, half-angle, and product-to-sum are all two-line corollaries, not new facts.

    Definition $$\sin(a \pm b) = \sin a\cos b \pm \cos a \sin b$$ $$\cos(a \pm b) = \cos a \cos b \mp \sin a \sin b$$ $$\tan(a \pm b) = \frac{\tan a \pm \tan b}{1 \mp \tan a \tan b}.$$ Mind the sign flip in the cosine formula.
    Worked example Exact $\sin 75^\circ$: $75 = 45 + 30$, so $$\sin 75^\circ = \sin45\cos30 + \cos45\sin30 = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} + \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\tfrac12 = \frac{\sqrt6 + \sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.9659.$$ (Calculator check: $\sin 75^\circ = 0.96593$. ✓)
    Think first Use an addition formula to explain the cofunction identity $\sin(\tfrac\pi2 - x) = \cos x$.
    Reveal
    $\sin(\tfrac\pi2 - x) = \sin\tfrac\pi2\cos x - \cos\tfrac\pi2\sin x = 1\cdot\cos x - 0\cdot\sin x = \cos x$. The "co" in cosine literally means "complement's sine".
    Where this goes Rotary position embeddings (Ch 14) rotate query/key pairs by position-dependent angles; the addition formulas are why relative position emerges from their dot products.
    Drill
    1. EASY Compute $\cos 15^\circ$ exactly using $45 - 30$.
      Solution
      $\cos45\cos30 + \sin45\sin30 = \frac{\sqrt6+\sqrt2}{4} \approx 0.9659$.
    2. MED Simplify $\sin(x + \pi)$.
      Solution
      $\sin x\cos\pi + \cos x \sin\pi = -\sin x$.
    3. HARD Given $\tan a = \tfrac12$, $\tan b = \tfrac13$, both acute. Find $a + b$ exactly.
      Solution
      $\tan(a+b) = \frac{\frac12 + \frac13}{1 - \frac16} = \frac{5/6}{5/6} = 1$. Both acute and each less than $45^\circ$, so $a + b = \tfrac\pi4$.
    4. HARD Prove $\cos(a-b) - \cos(a+b) = 2\sin a\sin b$.
      Solution
      $(\cos a\cos b + \sin a\sin b) - (\cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b) = 2\sin a\sin b$. $\blacksquare$

    Double-angle formulas

    Rotating by $a$ twice is rotating by $2a$: set $b = a$ in the addition formulas and you get the double-angle formulas — the workhorses for integrating $\sin^2$, for halving frequencies, and for the power-reduction tricks of calculus. Read $\cos 2a = 1 - 2\sin^2 a$ as a frequency statement: squaring a wave doubles its frequency and adds a constant offset. That is why a squared signal has a DC component — and why the average of $\sin^2$ over a period is exactly $\tfrac12$.

    Definition $$\sin 2a = 2\sin a\cos a$$ $$\cos 2a = \cos^2 a - \sin^2 a = 2\cos^2 a - 1 = 1 - 2\sin^2 a$$ $$\tan 2a = \frac{2\tan a}{1 - \tan^2 a}.$$ Power reduction (solve the $\cos 2a$ forms): $\cos^2 a = \frac{1 + \cos 2a}{2}$, $\sin^2 a = \frac{1 - \cos 2a}{2}$.
    Worked example $\sin\theta = \tfrac35$, $\theta$ acute. Then $\cos\theta = \tfrac45$ and: $\sin 2\theta = 2\cdot\tfrac35\cdot\tfrac45 = \tfrac{24}{25}$; $\cos 2\theta = 1 - 2\cdot\tfrac9{25} = \tfrac7{25}$. Check: $\big(\tfrac{24}{25}\big)^2 + \big(\tfrac7{25}\big)^2 = \tfrac{576+49}{625} = 1$. ✓
    Think first If $\sin\theta = \tfrac12$, is $\sin 2\theta = 1$?
    Reveal
    No — doubling the angle does not double (or otherwise simply scale) the sine. With $\theta = \tfrac\pi6$: $\sin 2\theta = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta = 2\cdot\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}2 = \tfrac{\sqrt3}2 \approx 0.866$, not 1. The cosine factor is the correction that memory tends to drop.
    Where this goes Power reduction $\sin^2 a = \frac{1-\cos 2a}{2}$ is how Ch 7 integrates $\sin^2$ and how RMS values of AC signals are computed; the "squaring doubles frequency" fact resurfaces in Ch 14 whenever a nonlinearity creates harmonics.
    Drill
    1. EASY If $\cos a = \tfrac1{\sqrt2}$ (acute $a$), compute $\cos 2a$.
      Solution
      $2\cdot\tfrac12 - 1 = 0$ (of course: $a = 45^\circ$, $2a = 90^\circ$).
    2. MED Express $\sin^2 x \cos^2 x$ with no powers of trig functions.
      Solution
      $(\sin x\cos x)^2 = \tfrac14\sin^2 2x = \tfrac14\cdot\frac{1 - \cos 4x}{2} = \frac{1 - \cos 4x}{8}$.
    3. HARD Solve $\sin 2x = \sin x$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      $2\sin x\cos x - \sin x = 0 \Rightarrow \sin x(2\cos x - 1) = 0$. $\sin x = 0$: $x = 0, \pi$. $\cos x = \tfrac12$: $x = \tfrac\pi3, \tfrac{5\pi}3$. Four solutions: $\{0, \tfrac\pi3, \pi, \tfrac{5\pi}3\}$.
    4. MED Given $\cos 2\theta = \tfrac{7}{25}$ with $\theta$ acute, find $\sin\theta$ and $\cos\theta$.
      Solution
      $\sin^2\theta = \frac{1 - \cos 2\theta}{2} = \frac{18/25}{2} = \frac{9}{25}$ and $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1 + 7/25}{2} = \frac{16}{25}$. Acute θ: $\sin\theta = \tfrac35$, $\cos\theta = \tfrac45$.

    Half-angle formulas

    Run power reduction backwards: knowing $\cos\theta$, you can get the sine and cosine of half the angle. This is how ancient astronomers built trig tables down to fractions of a degree, doubling precision by halving angles. Ptolemy's table, computed by hand in the second century, reached half-degree steps this way: start from the exact values you know, halve to get new exact values, and interpolate the gaps. The square roots stack up ($\cos 22.5^\circ$ has one, $\cos 11.25^\circ$ has two nested), but nothing beyond arithmetic is ever needed.

    Definition $$\sin\frac{a}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos a}{2}}, \quad \cos\frac{a}{2} = \pm\sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos a}{2}}, \quad \tan\frac{a}{2} = \frac{\sin a}{1 + \cos a} = \frac{1 - \cos a}{\sin a}.$$ The $\pm$ is fixed by which quadrant $\tfrac a2$ lands in (the tangent forms need no sign choice).
    Worked example Exact $\cos 22.5^\circ$: half of $45^\circ$. $$\cos 22.5^\circ = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \cos 45^\circ}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + \tfrac{\sqrt2}2}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}} = \frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt2}}{2} \approx 0.9239.$$ (Positive root: $22.5^\circ$ is in quadrant I.)
    Pitfall The $\pm$ sign is decided by the quadrant of $\tfrac a2$, not of $a$. Take $a = 300^\circ$ (quadrant IV, $\cos a = \tfrac12$): $\tfrac a2 = 150^\circ$ is in quadrant II, so $\sin\tfrac a2 = +\sqrt{\tfrac{1 - 1/2}{2}} = \tfrac12$ but $\cos\tfrac a2 = -\sqrt{\tfrac{1+1/2}{2}} = -\tfrac{\sqrt3}2$. Locate the half-angle first, then pick signs.
    Drill
    1. EASY Compute $\sin 15^\circ$ via half-angle of $30^\circ$.
      Solution
      $\sqrt{\frac{1 - \cos 30^\circ}{2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - \sqrt3/2}{2}} = \frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt3}}{2} \approx 0.2588$.
    2. MED $\cos a = -\tfrac13$ with $a \in (\pi/2, \pi)$. Find $\sin\tfrac a2$ and $\cos\tfrac a2$.
      Solution
      $\tfrac a2 \in (\tfrac\pi4, \tfrac\pi2)$: both positive. $\sin\tfrac a2 = \sqrt{\frac{1+\frac13}{2}} = \sqrt{\tfrac23} = \tfrac{\sqrt6}{3}$; $\cos\tfrac a2 = \sqrt{\frac{1-\frac13}{2}} = \sqrt{\tfrac13} = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}$.
    3. HARD Prove $\tan\tfrac a2 = \dfrac{\sin a}{1 + \cos a}$ from the double-angle formulas.
      Solution
      Let $t = \tfrac a2$. $\frac{\sin a}{1+\cos a} = \frac{2\sin t\cos t}{1 + 2\cos^2 t - 1} = \frac{2\sin t\cos t}{2\cos^2 t} = \frac{\sin t}{\cos t} = \tan t$. $\blacksquare$
    4. MED Compute $\tan 22.5^\circ$ exactly using the fraction form.
      Solution
      $\tan\frac{45^\circ}{2} = \frac{1 - \cos 45^\circ}{\sin 45^\circ} = \frac{1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}}{\frac{\sqrt2}{2}} = \frac{2 - \sqrt2}{\sqrt2} = \sqrt2 - 1 \approx 0.4142$. (Calculator: $\tan 22.5^\circ = 0.41421$. ✓)

    Product-to-sum identities

    Multiplying two waves produces a sum of two new waves at the sum and difference frequencies — the beat you hear when two guitar strings are slightly out of tune. These identities are Fourier analysis (Ch 14) in miniature: they are why frequencies mix the way they do. Multiply waves at frequencies $a$ and $b$ and you do not get "a wave at frequency $ab$" — you get equal parts $a-b$ and $a+b$. Every radio receiver, every audio effect called "ring modulation", and every aliasing artifact in a sampled signal is this one identity acting in the world.

    Definition $$\sin a\sin b = \tfrac12[\cos(a-b) - \cos(a+b)]$$ $$\cos a\cos b = \tfrac12[\cos(a-b) + \cos(a+b)]$$ $$\sin a\cos b = \tfrac12[\sin(a+b) + \sin(a-b)]$$ Sum-to-product (the reverse direction): $\sin a + \sin b = 2\sin\frac{a+b}{2}\cos\frac{a-b}{2}$, $\cos a + \cos b = 2\cos\frac{a+b}{2}\cos\frac{a-b}{2}$. None of these need memorizing under pressure: add and subtract the two angle-addition formulas and each one falls out in two lines.
    Worked example Two tuning forks at 440 Hz and 444 Hz: $\sin(2\pi\cdot440 t) + \sin(2\pi\cdot444 t) = 2\sin(2\pi\cdot 442 t)\cos(2\pi \cdot 2t)$ — a 442 Hz tone whose loudness envelope $|\cos(2\pi\cdot2t)|$ pulses at 4 beats per second (the envelope peaks twice per cosine cycle).
    Where this goes Orthogonality of sinusoids — the fact that $\int_0^{2\pi}\sin mx \sin nx\,dx = 0$ for $m \ne n$ — is proved in Ch 14 by product-to-sum: the product becomes two cosines that each average to zero over a full period. That orthogonality is what lets Fourier coefficients be read off one at a time.
    Drill
    1. EASY Write $\cos 5x\cos 3x$ as a sum.
      Solution
      $\tfrac12[\cos 2x + \cos 8x]$.
    2. MED Compute $\cos 75^\circ\cos 15^\circ$ exactly.
      Solution
      $\tfrac12[\cos 60^\circ + \cos 90^\circ] = \tfrac12[\tfrac12 + 0] = \tfrac14$.
    3. MED Compute $\sin 75^\circ \sin 15^\circ$ exactly.
      Solution
      $\tfrac12[\cos 60^\circ - \cos 90^\circ] = \tfrac12[\tfrac12 - 0] = \tfrac14$.
    4. HARD Solve $\sin 3x + \sin x = 0$ on $[0, \pi]$.
      Solution

    Inverse trigonometric functions

    "Which angle has sine 0.6?" has infinitely many answers, so to build an inverse function we agree on one canonical window of angles. Arcsine, arccosine and arctangent return the angle in that window — they turn ratios back into angles. The windows are chosen so each function is one-to-one there and covers every possible ratio: for sine and tangent a half-turn straddling zero, for cosine the top half of the circle. Everything confusing about inverse trig traces back to one fact: the trig function forgets which copy of the angle you started with, and the inverse can only hand back the canonical one.

    Definition $\arcsin x$: the $\theta \in [-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2]$ with $\sin\theta = x$ (domain $[-1,1]$). $\arccos x$: the $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ with $\cos\theta = x$ (domain $[-1,1]$). $\arctan x$: the $\theta \in (-\tfrac\pi2, \tfrac\pi2)$ with $\tan\theta = x$ (domain $\mathbb{R}$). Note $\sin(\arcsin x) = x$ always, but $\arcsin(\sin\theta) = \theta$ only when $\theta$ is already in the window.
    Worked example $\arcsin\tfrac12 = \tfrac\pi6$; $\arccos(-\tfrac12) = \tfrac{2\pi}3$; $\arctan(-1) = -\tfrac\pi4$. And a compound: $\cos(\arcsin\tfrac35)$ — draw the right triangle with opposite 3, hypotenuse 5: adjacent 4, answer $\tfrac45$ (positive, since arcsin outputs lie in quadrants I/IV where cosine ≥ 0).
    Pitfall $\arcsin(\sin\tfrac{5\pi}6) \ne \tfrac{5\pi}6$. Since $\sin\tfrac{5\pi}6 = \tfrac12$, the answer is $\tfrac\pi6$ — the inverse function snaps to its window.
    Where this goes In Ch 7 the inverse trig functions pay off spectacularly: $\frac{d}{dx}\arctan x = \frac{1}{1+x^2}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\arcsin x = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$, so these "angle recovery" functions are the antiderivatives of plain algebraic expressions — the reason $\int\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$ has an answer at all.
    Drill
    1. EASY $\arctan\sqrt3 = ?$
      Solution
      $\tfrac\pi3$.
    2. MED Simplify $\tan(\arccos x)$ for $0 < x \le 1$.
      Solution
      Triangle: adjacent $x$, hypotenuse 1, opposite $\sqrt{1-x^2}$: $\tan = \frac{\sqrt{1-x^2}}{x}$.
    3. MED Compute $\arccos(\cos 5)$ (radians).
      Solution
      $5$ rad lies in $(\pi, 2\pi)$, outside arccos's window $[0,\pi]$. Cosine is even and $2\pi$-periodic: $\cos 5 = \cos(-5) = \cos(2\pi - 5)$, and $2\pi - 5 \approx 1.283 \in [0, \pi]$. Answer: $2\pi - 5$.
    4. HARD Prove $\arcsin x + \arccos x = \tfrac\pi2$ for all $x \in [-1,1]$.
      Solution
      Let $\alpha = \arcsin x \in [-\tfrac\pi2,\tfrac\pi2]$. Then $\cos(\tfrac\pi2 - \alpha) = \sin\alpha = x$, and $\tfrac\pi2 - \alpha \in [0, \pi]$ — exactly arccos's window. So $\arccos x = \tfrac\pi2 - \alpha$. $\blacksquare$

    Trigonometric equations

    Unlike identities, equations hold only for special angles — your job is to find all of them. The method: isolate one trig function, find the solutions in one period using the unit circle, then add the period's multiples. A sanity habit worth building: before solving, sketch the wave and the horizontal line and count the crossings — you then know exactly how many answers to hunt for, and a missing or extra solution announces itself immediately.

    Definition General solutions: $\sin\theta = s$ ($|s|\le1$) $\Rightarrow \theta = \alpha + 2k\pi$ or $\theta = \pi - \alpha + 2k\pi$ where $\alpha = \arcsin s$. $\cos\theta = c \Rightarrow \theta = \pm\alpha + 2k\pi$, $\alpha = \arccos c$. $\tan\theta = t \Rightarrow \theta = \arctan t + k\pi$. ($k \in \mathbb{Z}$.)
    Worked example Solve $2\cos^2 x - \cos x - 1 = 0$ on $[0, 2\pi)$. Factor as a quadratic in $\cos x$: $(2\cos x + 1)(\cos x - 1) = 0$. $\cos x = 1$: $x = 0$. $\cos x = -\tfrac12$: $x = \tfrac{2\pi}3, \tfrac{4\pi}3$. Solutions: $\{0, \tfrac{2\pi}3, \tfrac{4\pi}3\}$. Graph check: the parabola-in-$\cos x$ view says $\cos x$ hits $1$ once per period (a tangency at the peak) and $-\tfrac12$ twice — three crossings, matching the three answers.
    Pitfall Never divide both sides by a trig factor that could be zero. In $\sin 2x = \sin x$, dividing by $\sin x$ silently deletes the solutions $x = 0, \pi$. Move everything to one side and factor instead — factoring keeps every root, division throws some away.
    Where this goes Ch 7's optimization sets derivatives to zero, and for anything oscillatory the resulting equation is a trig equation — finding the peak of $3\sin x + 4\cos x$ means solving $3\cos x = 4\sin x$, i.e. $\tan x = \tfrac34$, with exactly the machinery of this section.
    Drill
    1. EASY Solve $2\sin x - \sqrt3 = 0$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.

      $\sin x = \tfrac{\sqrt3}2$: $x = \tfrac\pi3, \tfrac{2\pi}3$.

      Solution

    3. MED Solve $\sin 3x = 1$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      $3x = \tfrac\pi2 + 2k\pi \Rightarrow x = \tfrac\pi6 + \tfrac{2k\pi}{3}$: $x = \tfrac\pi6, \tfrac{5\pi}6, \tfrac{3\pi}2$.
    4. HARD Solve $\sin x + \cos x = 1$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
      Solution
      Write left side as $\sqrt2\sin(x + \tfrac\pi4)$. Then $\sin(x+\tfrac\pi4) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}$: $x + \tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac\pi4 + 2k\pi$ or $\tfrac{3\pi}4 + 2k\pi$, so $x = 0$ or $x = \tfrac\pi2$. Check both: $0+1 = 1$ ✓, $1+0 = 1$ ✓.

    Law of sines

    In any triangle, each side is proportional to the sine of the angle staring at it. Big angle, big opposite side — the law of sines makes that exact, and lets you solve triangles that have no right angle. The shared constant $2R$ gives the picture: inscribe the triangle in its circumscribed circle, and each ratio $a/\sin A$ measures the same circle's diameter — three different windows onto one hidden quantity.

    Definition In a triangle with sides $a, b, c$ opposite angles $A, B, C$: $$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R,$$ where $R$ is the circumradius. Use it given ASA/AAS (two angles + a side) or SSA — but SSA is the ambiguous case: zero, one, or two triangles may fit. (Why ambiguous? Because $\sin B = \sin(180^\circ - B)$: the sine ratio cannot tell an acute angle from its obtuse supplement.)
    Worked example $A = 40^\circ$, $B = 60^\circ$, $a = 10$. Then $C = 80^\circ$ and $$b = \frac{a\sin B}{\sin A} = \frac{10 \times 0.8660}{0.6428} \approx 13.47, \qquad c = \frac{10\times\sin 80^\circ}{0.6428} = \frac{9.848}{0.6428} \approx 15.32.$$
    Pitfall Ambiguous case: given $a = 7$, $b = 10$, $A = 40^\circ$, we get $\sin B = \frac{b \sin A}{a} = \frac{10(0.643)}{7} \approx 0.918$ — and both $B \approx 66.7^\circ$ and $B \approx 113.3^\circ$ give valid triangles. Always test the obtuse candidate: write down both $B = \arcsin(\cdot)$ and $180^\circ - \arcsin(\cdot)$, and keep each one whose angles still sum below $180^\circ$.
    Drill
    1. EASY $A = 30^\circ$, $a = 5$, $B = 90^\circ$. Find $b$.
      Solution
      $b = \frac{5\sin 90^\circ}{\sin 30^\circ} = \frac{5}{0.5} = 10$.
    2. MED $a = 3$, $b = 10$, $A = 40^\circ$. How many triangles fit?
      Solution
      $\sin B = \frac{10\sin 40^\circ}{3} = \frac{10(0.643)}{3} \approx 2.14 > 1$ — impossible. Zero triangles: side $a$ is too short to reach across from that angle.
    3. MED $a = 8$, $A = 35^\circ$, $b = 12$. How many triangles exist?

    4. MED A triangle has $a = 6$, $A = 30^\circ$. What is the diameter of its circumscribed circle?
      Solution
      The extended law of sines gives it directly: $2R = \frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{6}{1/2} = 12$. No other side or angle needed — one side and its opposite angle pin down the circle.
    5. HARD Two observers 1 km apart both see a balloon between them. Elevation angles: $55^\circ$ and $70^\circ$. How high is the balloon?
      Solution
      Triangle with base 1 km, base angles $55^\circ, 70^\circ$, apex $180-125 = 55^\circ$. Height $= d\sin 55^\circ = 1.147\times 0.8192 \approx 0.940$ km. (Or directly $h = \frac{\sin 70^\circ \sin 55^\circ}{\sin 55^\circ} = \sin 70^\circ \approx 0.940$ km — a neat coincidence here because apex $=55^\circ$.)

    Law of cosines

    The Pythagorean theorem with a correction term for non-right angles: the $-2ab\cos C$ term punishes wide angles and rewards narrow ones. We proved it with dot products in Ch 5; here it solves SAS and SSS triangles. Watch the correction term work: $C < 90^\circ$ makes $\cos C$ positive and shortens $c$ below the Pythagorean value; $C > 90^\circ$ makes it negative and stretches $c$ beyond. The formula is the dot product $\|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|\mathbf{a}\|^2 + \|\mathbf{b}\|^2 - 2\,\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}$ wearing triangle clothes.

    Definition $$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C,$$ and cyclically for the other sides. Solve for an angle: $\cos C = \dfrac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}$. When $C = 90^\circ$ the correction vanishes — Pythagoras is the special case.
    Worked example Sides $a = 5$, $b = 8$, included angle $C = 60^\circ$: $c^2 = 25 + 64 - 2\cdot5\cdot8\cdot\tfrac12 = 89 - 40 = 49$, so $c = 7$. Then the angle opposite $a$: $\cos A = \frac{64 + 49 - 25}{2\cdot8\cdot7} = \frac{88}{112} = \tfrac{11}{14}$, $A \approx 38.2^\circ$.
    Think first Can a triangle have sides 2, 3, and 7? What does the law of cosines say if you try?
    Reveal
    No: $2 + 3 < 7$ violates the triangle inequality. The formula agrees loudly: $\cos C = \frac{4 + 9 - 49}{2\cdot2\cdot3} = \frac{-36}{12} = -3$, and no angle has cosine $-3$. The law of cosines contains the triangle inequality as the statement $|\cos C| \le 1$.
    Where this goes Solving $\cos C = \frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}$ is exactly the angle-between-vectors computation of Ch 8's inner-product spaces — and in high dimensions, cosine of the angle between random vectors concentrates near 0, a fact Ch 12 uses constantly.

    Polar coordinates

    Instead of "how far east, how far north", polar coordinates say "how far, and in what direction": a distance $r$ and an angle $\theta$. Anything circular, spiral, or rotational is simpler in polar — and complex numbers wear polar form as their native dress. The two systems are a division of labor: Cartesian makes translation easy and rotation awkward; polar makes rotation trivial (add to θ) and translation awkward. Choosing coordinates to match a problem's symmetry is one of the oldest and best tricks in applied mathematics.

    Definition Polar $(r, \theta)$ ↔ Cartesian $(x, y)$: $$x = r\cos\theta, \quad y = r\sin\theta; \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}, \quad \theta = \operatorname{atan2}(y, x).$$ Representation is not unique: $(r, \theta) = (r, \theta + 2\pi k)$, and $(-r, \theta) = (r, \theta + \pi)$. A polar curve $r = f(\theta)$ plots a radius for each direction. Here $\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)$ is the two-argument arctangent every math library provides: it returns the angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$, using the signs of $x$ and $y$ separately to pick the correct quadrant.
    Worked example Convert $(x,y) = (-3, 3)$: $r = \sqrt{9+9} = 3\sqrt2$; the point is in quadrant II, $\theta = \pi - \tfrac\pi4 = \tfrac{3\pi}4$. So $(3\sqrt2, \tfrac{3\pi}4)$. Beware: $\arctan\frac{3}{-3} = \arctan(-1) = -\tfrac\pi4$ is the wrong quadrant — this is exactly why atan2 exists.
    Where this goes Ch 9's change of variables turns double integrals over disks into polar form with the Jacobian factor $r\,dr\,d\theta$ — the move that cracks the Gaussian integral $\int e^{-x^2}dx = \sqrt\pi$, the normalizing constant of every normal distribution in Ch 11.

    Parametric equations

    Instead of $y$ as a function of $x$, let both coordinates be functions of a clock variable $t$: a moving point tracing a path. Parametric curves can loop, cross themselves, and go vertical — everything function graphs cannot do. Every trajectory in physics and every generated sampling path is parametric. The mental upgrade: stop asking "what is $y$ at this $x$?" and start asking "where is the point at this time?" — one degree of freedom driving two (or a thousand) coordinates. A curve in embedding space traced during optimization, the path of a diffusion sample, a robot arm's motion: all parametric curves with $t$ as the clock.

    Definition A parametric curve is $(x, y) = (f(t), g(t))$ for $t$ in an interval. The circle: $(\cos t, \sin t)$, $t \in [0, 2\pi)$. The ellipse: $(a\cos t, b\sin t)$. Eliminating $t$ (when possible) recovers a Cartesian equation, but loses the direction and speed of travel.
    Pitfall Eliminating the parameter can silently enlarge the curve. $x = t^2$, $y = t^4$ satisfies $y = x^2$, but the parametric curve is only the right half of that parabola ($x \ge 0$ always). After eliminating $t$, go back and ask which part of the Cartesian curve the parameter actually reaches.
    Worked example A projectile: $x = 20t$, $y = 15t - 4.9t^2$. Eliminate $t = x/20$: $y = \tfrac{15}{20}x - 4.9\tfrac{x^2}{400} = 0.75x - 0.01225x^2$ — a parabola. It lands when $y = 0$: $t(15 - 4.9t) = 0 \Rightarrow t \approx 3.06$ s, at $x \approx 61.2$ m.
    Where this goes Ch 7 differentiates parametric curves componentwise: velocity $(x'(t), y'(t))$, speed $\sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2}$. Ch 27's diffusion models generate images by following a parametric path through pixel space — the "curve" is the denoising trajectory and $t$ is the noise level.

    Euler's formula

    Euler's formula says the exponential function, fed an imaginary number, walks around the unit circle: $e^{i\theta}$ is the point at angle θ. Suddenly all of trigonometry becomes exponent algebra — the addition formulas are just $e^{ia}e^{ib} = e^{i(a+b)}$. The right mental model: multiplying by $e^{i\theta}$ is rotating by θ. That one sentence converts every rotation problem into multiplication, every wave into a spinning arrow whose shadow on the real axis is the cosine you see, and every trig identity into bookkeeping you could rediscover in the margin.

    Definition $$e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.$$ Consequences: $|e^{i\theta}| = 1$; $\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta}}{2}$, $\sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta} - e^{-i\theta}}{2i}$; De Moivre: $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^n = \cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta$; any complex number: $z = re^{i\theta}$ (polar form, Ch 2). Justification: the Taylor series of $e^x$ (Ch 7) at $x = i\theta$ splits into cosine's and sine's series.
    Worked example Derive the angle-addition formulas in one line: $$e^{i(a+b)} = e^{ia}e^{ib} = (\cos a + i\sin a)(\cos b + i\sin b) = (\cos a\cos b - \sin a\sin b) + i(\sin a\cos b + \cos a\sin b).$$ Matching real and imaginary parts against $\cos(a+b) + i\sin(a+b)$ gives both formulas at once.
    Pitfall $e^{i\theta}$ never grows: $|e^{i\theta}| = 1$ for every real θ. The exponential explodes only along the real direction — in general $e^{a+ib} = e^a(\cos b + i\sin b)$, so the real part of the exponent sets the magnitude and the imaginary part only sets the direction. "Exponential growth" and "rotation" are the two faces of one function.
    Where this goes Complex exponentials are the basis functions of the Fourier transform (Ch 14) and the reason rotary position embeddings (Ch 14) are phase rotations. Multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$ is also the $2\times 2$ rotation matrix of Ch 8 in disguise: the map $z \mapsto e^{i\theta}z$ acts on $(x,y)$ exactly as $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ does.
    Drill
    1. EASY Compute $e^{i\pi/2}$.
      Solution
      $\cos\tfrac\pi2 + i\sin\tfrac\pi2 = i$ — a quarter turn from 1. Multiplying by $i$ is rotating by $90^\circ$, which is why $i^2 = -1$: two quarter turns make a half turn.
    2. MED Use De Moivre to find $\cos 3\theta$ in terms of $\cos\theta$.
      Solution
      $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^3$: real part $= \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta\sin^2\theta = \cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta(1-\cos^2\theta) = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$.
    3. HARD Compute $(1 + i)^{10}$ using polar form.
      Solution
      $1 + i = \sqrt2 e^{i\pi/4}$. Tenth power: $(\sqrt2)^{10} e^{i10\pi/4} = 32\, e^{i5\pi/2} = 32 e^{i\pi/2} = 32i$.
    4. MED Using $\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}$, prove the power-reduction identity $\cos^2\theta = \frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}$.
      Solution
      $\cos^2\theta = \Big(\frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}\Big)^2 = \frac{e^{2i\theta} + 2 + e^{-2i\theta}}{4} = \frac{2\cos 2\theta + 2}{4} = \frac{1 + \cos 2\theta}{2}$. $\blacksquare$ Squaring in the exponential picture visibly creates the frequencies $2\theta$, $0$, $-2\theta$ — nothing else.

    Euler's identity

    Set $\theta = \pi$ in Euler's formula and five of the most fundamental constants in mathematics — $e$, $i$, $\pi$, 1, 0 — collapse into one equation. It is not magic once you see it as geometry: walking half-way around the unit circle lands you at $-1$. The equation is a summary of everything this chapter built — angle measure (π radians is half a turn), the unit circle (where the walk happens), and Euler's formula (why an exponential does the walking). If you can explain to someone why it is true in one sentence, the chapter has done its job.

    Definition $$e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0.$$ Read: rotating by $\pi$ radians is multiplication by $-1$. More generally $e^{i\pi k} = (-1)^k$, and the $n$-th roots of unity $e^{2\pi i k/n}$, $k = 0,\dots,n-1$, sit at the vertices of a regular $n$-gon on the unit circle.
    Worked example The cube roots of unity: $e^{2\pi i k/3}$ for $k=0,1,2$: $1$, $-\tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$, $-\tfrac12 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$. Their sum: $1 - \tfrac12 - \tfrac12 + i(\tfrac{\sqrt3}2 - \tfrac{\sqrt3}2) = 0$ — the vertices of a regular polygon always balance at the center.
    Think first What is $i^i$? (Yes, it's a real number.)
    Reveal
    $i = e^{i\pi/2}$, so $i^i = e^{i\cdot i\pi/2} = e^{-\pi/2} \approx 0.2079$. (One value; other branches give $e^{-\pi/2 + 2\pi k}$.)
    Drill
    1. EASY Evaluate $e^{2\pi i}$.
      Solution
      Full turn: $1$.
    2. MED Find all solutions of $z^4 = 1$ and plot them mentally.
      Solution
      $z = e^{2\pi ik/4}$: $1, i, -1, -i$ — the corners of a square on the unit circle.
    3. MED What is $\ln(-1)$, if we allow complex answers?
      Solution
      We need $e^z = -1$. Euler's identity gives $e^{i\pi} = -1$, so $z = i\pi$ works — and so does $i\pi + 2\pi i k$ for any integer $k$, since a full extra turn changes nothing. The principal value is $\ln(-1) = i\pi$: the logarithm of a negative number is not "undefined", it is imaginary.
    4. HARD Show that $\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} e^{2\pi i k/n} = 0$ for $n \ge 2$.
      Solution
      Geometric series with ratio $\omega = e^{2\pi i/n} \ne 1$: sum $= \frac{\omega^n - 1}{\omega - 1} = \frac{1 - 1}{\omega - 1} = 0$. $\blacksquare$ (This orthogonality is the engine of the discrete Fourier transform, Ch 14.)
    $e^{i\pi/3}$ equals…
    $e^{i\pi/3} = \cos\tfrac\pi3 + i\sin\tfrac\pi3 = \tfrac12 + \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}i$: the point at $60^\circ$ on the unit circle. The second option is the $30^\circ$ point, the third is $120^\circ$.

    1. MED A drone flies from the origin 100 m at bearing N40°E (40° east of north), then 80 m at bearing S70°E. Find its final position (east, north) and straight-line distance from start.
      Solution
      Leg 1: east $100\sin 40^\circ \approx 64.28$, north $100\cos 40^\circ \approx 76.60$. Leg 2 (S70°E: 70° east of south): east $80\sin 70^\circ \approx 75.18$, north $-80\cos 70^\circ \approx -27.36$. Total: east $\approx 139.46$, north $\approx 49.25$. Distance $= \sqrt{139.46^2 + 49.25^2} = \sqrt{19449 + 2426} \approx \sqrt{21875} \approx 147.9$ m.
    2. HARD Prove that for any triangle inscribed in a circle of radius $R$, the area is $\dfrac{abc}{4R}$.
      Solution
      Area $= \tfrac12 ab\sin C$ (two sides and the included angle: height from $B$ onto side $b$... concretely, height $= a\sin C$ onto base $b$). Law of sines: $\frac{c}{\sin C} = 2R \Rightarrow \sin C = \frac{c}{2R}$. Substitute: Area $= \tfrac12 ab\cdot\frac{c}{2R} = \frac{abc}{4R}$. $\blacksquare$
    3. HARD Signal mixing: an AM radio multiplies a message $m(t) = \cos(2\pi\cdot 1000\, t)$ with a carrier $c(t) = \cos(2\pi \cdot 500000\, t)$. (a) Which frequencies does $m(t)c(t)$ contain? (b) At the receiver we multiply again by the carrier. Show the result contains the original 1000 Hz tone (plus high-frequency junk a filter removes), with amplitude reduced.
      Solution
      (a) Product-to-sum: $\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]$: frequencies $499\,000$ and $501\,000$ Hz. (b) Multiply by $\cos(2\pi\cdot500000 t)$ again: each sideband splits: $\tfrac12\cos(2\pi\,499000 t)\cos(2\pi\,500000t) = \tfrac14[\cos(2\pi\,1000t) + \cos(2\pi\,999000t)]$, and similarly the 501 kHz band gives $\tfrac14[\cos(2\pi\,1000 t) + \cos(2\pi\,1001000 t)]$. Sum: $\tfrac12\cos(2\pi\,1000t)$ + terms near 1 MHz. Low-pass filtering leaves the message at half amplitude. This is exactly how frequency mixing — and the convolution theorem of Ch 14 — works.

    Task 1 — Triangle solver. Write solve_sas(a, C_deg, b) returning the third side and remaining angles using the laws of cosines and sines. Verify on the worked example: a=5, C=60°, b=8 → c=7, A≈38.21°, B≈81.79°.

    Reference solution
    import numpy as np

def solve_sas(a, C_deg, b):
    C = np.radians(C_deg)
    c = np.sqrt(a*a + b*b - 2*a*b*np.cos(C))
    # find remaining angles with law of cosines (robust, no ambiguity)
    A = np.degrees(np.arccos((b*b + c*c - a*a) / (2*b*c)))
    B = 180.0 - C_deg - A
    return c, A, B

c, A, B = solve_sas(5, 60, 8)
print(c, A, B)      # 7.0  38.213...  81.786...
assert abs(A + B + 60 - 180) < 1e-9

    Task 2 — Beats. Sample $f(t)=\sin(2\pi\,440t)+\sin(2\pi\,444t)$ at 8 kHz for 1 s. Compute the envelope's beat frequency empirically by counting the maxima of a smoothed $|f|$, and confirm it is 4 Hz as the sum-to-product identity predicts.

    Reference solution
    import numpy as np

fs = 8000
t = np.arange(0, 1, 1/fs)
f = np.sin(2*np.pi*440*t) + np.sin(2*np.pi*444*t)

# envelope = |2 cos(2 pi * 2 t)|; recover by rectify + moving average
rect = np.abs(f)
win = int(fs * 0.01)                     # 10 ms window kills the 442 Hz ripple
env = np.convolve(rect, np.ones(win)/win, mode='same')

# count peaks of the envelope
peaks = np.where((env[1:-1] > env[:-2]) & (env[1:-1] > env[2:]))[0]
# keep well-separated peaks only
sep = peaks[np.insert(np.diff(peaks) > win, 0, True)]
print(len(sep))                          # ~4 beats in 1 second
# theory: sum-to-product gives envelope |2cos(2 pi 2 t)| -> 4 maxima/s

    Task 3 — Rotation by complex multiplication. Represent 2D points as complex numbers and implement rotation about the origin as multiplication by $e^{i\theta}$. Rotate the square with corners $\pm1 \pm i$ by 30° and check that lengths and the dot products between corner vectors are preserved.

    Reference solution
    import numpy as np

corners = np.array([1+1j, -1+1j, -1-1j, 1-1j])
theta = np.radians(30)
rotated = corners * np.exp(1j*theta)     # Euler: e^{i t} rotates by t

print(np.abs(corners))                   # all sqrt(2)
print(np.abs(rotated))                   # unchanged
# dot product of vectors u,v as complex numbers: Re(u * conj(v))
def dot(u, v): return (u * np.conj(v)).real
for i in range(4):
    u1, v1 = corners[i], corners[(i+1) % 4]
    u2, v2 = rotated[i], rotated[(i+1) % 4]
    assert np.isclose(dot(u1, v1), dot(u2, v2))   # angles preserved
print(rotated[0])                        # (0.366+1.366j) = rot of 1+i
